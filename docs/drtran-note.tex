\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This document describes \texttt{drtran}, a program that estimates Box--Jenkins
transfer function models by exact maximum likelihood. A transfer function model
is recast as a vector ARMA (VARMA) with diagonal autoregressive, moving-average
and covariance matrices, so that a direct exact VARMA likelihood applies without
modification. We state the transformation formally, document its implementation
in the command-line program, and illustrate it on two applications: the
pass-through of crude oil to consumer prices, and a recursive multivariate model
of employment by sector. The document also records an identification result that
is easy to overlook in practice: a contemporaneous transfer coefficient
$\omega_0$ and the corresponding innovation covariance $\sigma_{12}$ are exactly
observationally equivalent when the noise and the input share the same
autoregressive operator, and are separated otherwise only by a signal
proportional to the difference between those operators. Every numerical result
is reproducible from the program's test battery.
\end{abstract}

\section{Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

\texttt{drtran} estimates the transfer function models of
Box, Jenkins and Reinsel~\cite[chs.~10--11]{bjr1994} by exact maximum
likelihood. Its distinguishing choice is representational: rather than build the
noise series by subtraction and hand it to a univariate routine, the program
casts the whole transfer function model as a VARMA and evaluates the exact
likelihood of that VARMA directly. Three properties follow from the cast and
organize the rest of the document.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The model class.} A single-output transfer function model with $k$
  exogenous inputs is a VARMA of $m=k+1$ series with \emph{diagonal}
  autoregressive, moving-average and innovation-covariance matrices; all the
  coupling lives in the first equation. The representation is standard
  \cite{tiaobox1981,reinsel1997,lutkepohl2005}; \S\ref{sec:theory} states it as a
  premultiplication and records the properties that its implementation depends
  on.

\item \textbf{The extension to a network.} A series may be simultaneously the
  output of one relation and the input of another. \texttt{drtran} generalizes
  the cast to a directed acyclic graph of transfers (\S\ref{sec:network}): a
  recursive dynamic system, or Wold causal chain, that the single-output theory
  does not cover but the diagonal VARMA accommodates without change.

\item \textbf{Exactness of the pre-sample.} Because each input is modelled as a
  series \emph{of the system}, with its own ARMA, its pre-sample is integrated
  out by the exact likelihood at no cost in parameters (\S\ref{sec:truncation}).
  The number reported as the log-likelihood is then the exact likelihood of the
  observed data, so likelihood-ratio tests and information criteria are valid.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The theoretical content is elementary and standard \cite{lutkepohl2005,
reinsel1997, gomez2019}; the reduction of a structured model to a plain VARMA so
that a direct exact likelihood applies unchanged is the device of
Mauricio~\cite{mauricio2006}. What this document contributes is the concrete
combination and its implementation: an automatic reduction from a univariate
model specification to a VARMA structure, a declarative language for parameter
restrictions (\S\ref{sec:restrictions}), the network extension, and the
identification analysis of \S\ref{sec:identification}. Every figure below is
produced by the program's test battery.

\subsection{Lineage}

The program sits at the confluence of two lines of software, both of which
predate it. On one side, univariate ARIMA and generalized-seasonal models with
intervention analysis, in the Box--Jenkins tradition (the program \texttt{fue},
built on M\'elard's exact univariate likelihood~\cite{melard1984}). On the
other, the exact likelihood of an arbitrary stationary
VARMA~\cite{mauricio1995,mauricio1997} and its implementation \texttt{drvarma}.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Box, Jenkins \& Reinsel~\cite{bjr1994} & the models \\
\quad \texttt{fue} & univariate ARIMA/generalized-seasonal with intervention \\
Mauricio~\cite{mauricio1995,mauricio1997} & exact likelihood of a VARMA \\
\quad \texttt{drvarma} & estimates arbitrary VARMA models \\
\quad\quad \texttt{drtran} & the bridge: univariate $\rightarrow$ VARMA $\rightarrow$ exact likelihood \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
\texttt{drtran} automates the joining of the two: it reads a univariate model
specification for each series, casts the transfer function system into the VARMA
form the likelihood engine expects, and estimates every parameter---each series'
ARMA, the transfers, the deterministic terms and the means---in a single
maximization. A design invariant makes the bridge testable: when no transfer is
present, the joint fit must reproduce the univariate program run separately on
each series, to full machine precision. This homologation is the entry gate of
the test battery, and \S\ref{sec:separability} explains why it must hold exactly.

\subsection{The cast}

A transfer function model
\begin{equation}
Y_t \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{\omega_j(B)}{\delta_j(B)}\,B^{b_j}\,X_{j,t}
        \;+\; N_t
\label{eq:tf}
\end{equation}
is recast as a VARMA of $m = 1+k$ series in which
\begin{align}
w_{t,1} &= w^{Y}_t - \textstyle\sum_j \nu_j(B)\, w^{X_j}_t \qquad (\text{the noise } N),\\
w_{t,j+1} &= w^{X_j}_t,
\end{align}
with $\Phi$ and $\Theta$ \emph{diagonal} (each series carries its own ARMA) and
$Q$ diagonal. All of the coupling lives in the \emph{subtracted} transfer, so the
VARMA that the likelihood engine sees has no off-diagonal element anywhere.

Two consequences follow, and they organize the implementation:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The exact likelihood engine is used \textbf{unmodified}. Any discrepancy
  between the joint fit and the univariate program is, by construction, a defect
  of the bridge rather than of the estimator.
\item The joint fit \textbf{reproduces} the univariate program run separately on
  each series, to machine precision, across a range of specifications
  (deterministic seasonality; the airline model; generalized seasonality with
  fixed-frequency factors; a fixed autoregressive coefficient). This homologation
  is the entry gate of the test battery, and \S\ref{sec:separability} explains why
  it must hold exactly.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Estimation choices, and their consequences}

\subsection{The input model: fixed, or estimated jointly}
\label{sec:separability}

In the classical procedure the univariate model of an input is identified once and
held fixed thereafter~\cite[chs.~10--11]{bjr1994}. \texttt{drtran} instead
estimates every parameter---the output's ARMA, each input's ARMA, the transfers,
the deterministic terms and the means---in a single maximization. The two agree
exactly whenever the innovation covariance is diagonal, as the following case
illustrates. On Spanish CPI $\leftarrow$ WTI crude:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & input ARMA \emph{fixed} & input ARMA \emph{free} \\
\midrule
$\log L$   & $-718.183933$ & $-718.183933$ \\
$\omega_0$ & $0.016402$    & $0.016402$ \\
$\omega_1$ & $0.010792$    & $0.010792$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The estimates are identical, and this is not a coincidence. With $Q$ diagonal and
no feedback, the joint likelihood factorizes as $L(N)\cdot L(X)$, and $L(X)$ does
not involve the transfer. Holding the input model fixed is therefore not an
approximation but exactly optimal in that case; it is also why the joint fit
homologates with the univariate program. The factorization breaks the moment
$\sigma_{12}$ is freed---the case in which a bivariate relation is specified
through the innovation covariance rather than a transfer (\S\ref{sec:identification}).
There, joint estimation is no longer optional.

\subsection{The scale normalization $\sigma^2 Q$}
\label{sec:qq}

The engine assumes $a_t \sim N(0,\sigma^2 Q)$ and concentrates $\sigma^2$ out. The
decomposition $\Sigma=\sigma^2 Q$ is not unique; this raises no problem for the
estimation of $\Sigma=E[a_t a_t']$, since interest lies in the product, but it does
affect inference on $Q$ alone~\cite[p.~474]{mauricio2002}. The concentrated
criterion is~\cite[eq.~12]{mauricio2002}
\begin{equation}
F(\beta\mid w) \;=\; \bigl(\tilde{w}' R_W^{-1}\tilde{w}\bigr)\,
                     |R_W|^{1/(mn)},
\label{eq:crit}
\end{equation}
and under $Q\to cQ$ the matrix $R_W$ scales by $c$: the first factor goes as
$1/c$, the second as $c$. \emph{$F$ is exactly invariant.} Since $\sigma^2$ is
concentrated, $\hat\sigma^2(cQ)\cdot cQ = \hat\sigma^2(Q)\cdot Q$: the product
does not move. Estimating the same model with the scale of $Q$ free and with it
normalized to $Q_{11}=1$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & scale free & $Q_{11}=1$ \\
\midrule
$\log L$                         & $-767.424341$ & $-767.424341$ \\
$\hat\Sigma$                     & $0.062666 \,/\, 68.838114$ & $0.062666 \,/\, 68.838114$ \\
$\mathrm{SE}(\phi_1)$            & $0.062204$ & $0.062204$ \\
$\mathrm{SE}(\mu)$               & $0.028502$ & $0.028502$ \\
$\mathrm{SE}(\log \mathrm{var}_2/\mathrm{var}_1)$ & $\mathbf{0.193083}$ & $\mathbf{0.136399}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The flat direction is exact, and it is harmless for $\log L$, for $\hat\Sigma$,
and for the standard errors of $\phi,\theta,\mu,\omega,\delta$. The consequence is
confined to inference on $Q$ itself: the scale parameter, which is unidentified,
receives a plausible-looking $t=-0.19$ with $p=0.85$; and the variance ratio,
which is identified, comes out with a standard error inflated by $42\%$.

\texttt{drtran} normalizes $Q_{11}=1$ because deciding whether a covariance is
zero is a hypothesis test, and the standard error that test rests on must be the
identified one.

\subsection{Standard errors: exact Hessian, not the accumulated one}
\label{sec:hessian}

Standard errors may be computed either from the BFGS-accumulated Hessian or from a
finite-difference Hessian of the concentrated criterion. \texttt{drtran} uses the
latter. A BFGS Hessian is built from the steps the optimizer takes; it never sees
the directions in which the optimizer does not move, and so produces finite numbers
where there should be none. The effect is visible in two ways: standard errors
computed from the accumulated Hessian are unstable---two runs of the same model,
with identical point estimates, can report $\mathrm{SE}(\mu)=0.073304$ and
$0.028316$; and the standard errors so obtained for the elements of $Q$ lie along
the flat direction of \S\ref{sec:qq}. With the finite-difference Hessian,
$\mathrm{SE}(\mu)=0.028502$, the exact GLS value.

\subsection{From a single output to a network}
\label{sec:network}

The classical apparatus covers a single output driven by $k$ exogenous
inputs~\cite[chs.~10--11]{bjr1994}. Many systems of interest exceed that boundary:
a series may be at once the output of one relation and the input of another. In the
employment model of \S\ref{sec:m6}, for instance, construction employment is an
output of services employment and an input to industrial employment,
\begin{center}
\texttt{EC} $\xrightarrow{\;b=2\;}$ \texttt{EU} $\xrightarrow{\;b=1\;}$
\texttt{EI} $\xrightarrow{\;b=1\;}$ \texttt{EP}\,,
\end{center}
a recursive dynamic system. \texttt{drtran} generalizes the cast to a
\emph{directed acyclic graph} of transfers, declared in a file: each link subtracts
its transfer from \emph{its} output, so a series may be both a target and a source;
the VARMA stays diagonal; and forecasting walks the graph in topological order,
propagating the system's $\psi$ weights,
\begin{equation}
\Psi_{ij}(B) \;=\; \delta_{ij}\,\psi_i(B)
   \;+\; \sum_{k:\,\mathrm{out}(k)=i} \nu_k(B)\,\Psi_{\mathrm{in}(k),\,j}(B),
\end{equation}
so that the forecast error of a series inherits the innovations of everything
upstream of it, each filtered by the $\nu(B)$ it crosses. A \emph{cycle} is
rejected: the system would be simultaneous and cannot be triangularized by
subtraction.

Measured on a synthetic chain $X \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y$: the network
recovers both transfers and, \textbf{with the same seven free parameters}, beats
the star by $213$ points of log-likelihood ($-1038.6$ vs $-1251.8$). In the star,
the direct link $X\rightarrow Y$ that it is forced to postulate is insignificant
($t=1.45$)---because the effect of $X$ on $Y$ is \emph{indirect}, and only the
network can say so.

\subsection{Restrictions: declared, not coded}
\label{sec:restrictions}

Parameter restrictions across the equations of a system---a transfer denominator
that equals the input's own autoregressive operator, a factorized numerator whose
coefficients share a parameter, an accounting identity linking deterministic
terms---were traditionally imposed by writing the constrained coefficients into the
estimation code by hand. \texttt{drtran} declares them instead in a \emph{slot
table}: every structural parameter, referred to by the name the program prints, is
one of

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
\textsc{free} & estimated \\
\textsc{fixed} & held at a stated value \\
\textsc{alias} & set equal to another parameter (shared) \\
\textsc{product} & the product of two parameters, \texttt{x = y * z} \\
\textsc{lincomb} & a signed sum of terms, each a parameter or a product,
   \texttt{x = y*z - w} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Aliasing expresses a shared coefficient; the product and linear-combination forms
express factorized numerators, which arise whenever a transfer inherits part of the
dynamics of its input (\S\ref{sec:m6}). The gradient of a derived slot is obtained
by finite differences over the free parameters, so no chain rule is coded. These
are the \emph{linear} and \emph{multiplicative} restrictions that a factorized
system needs; a genuinely nonlinear constraint such as a unit long-run gain
$g=\nu(1)=1$ is not \emph{imposed} by the slot table, but it can be \emph{tested}
directly once the model is fitted (\S\ref{sec:gain}).

\subsection{The start of the sample: neither truncation nor backforecasting}
\label{sec:truncation}

The transfer $\sum_k \nu_k X_{t-k}$ needs the input's past, and at $t=1$ there is
none. The classical solution is to \emph{backforecast} it~\cite[chs.~6,~11]{bjr1994}.
A na\"ive alternative is to \emph{truncate}: to assume a zero pre-sample input. The
exact likelihood makes backforecasting unnecessary rather than merely inferior, but
only when the transfer is kept inside the model. Two distinct problems must be
separated:

\begin{description}
\item[The noise's initialisation.] Box and Jenkins backforecast the pre-sample
  \emph{innovations}. The exact likelihood computes this analytically. Obsolete,
  as one would expect after thirty years.
\item[The input's pre-sample values.] A different problem entirely. When the noise
  is built \emph{outside} the engine, as $N_t = w_{Y,t} - \sum_k \nu_k
  w_{X,t-k}$, the engine never sees the inputs: it is handed a noise series that
  is already contaminated at the start of the sample. \textbf{No amount of exactness
  downstream can repair that.}
\end{description}

The fix is not to backforecast. It is to stop subtracting. Writing
\[
\delta(B)\,w_{Y,t}=\omega(B)B^{b} w_{X,t}+\delta(B)N_t,
\qquad \phi_N(B)N_t=\theta_N(B)a_{N,t},
\]
and multiplying through by $\phi_N(B)$ gives a restricted VARMA,
\begin{equation}
\bigl[\phi_N\delta\bigr](B)\,w_{Y,t}
\;-\;\bigl[\phi_N\,\omega\,B^{b}\bigr](B)\,w_{X,t}
\;=\;\bigl[\delta\,\theta_N\bigr](B)\,a_{N,t},
\label{eq:embed}
\end{equation}
whose series are handed to the engine \emph{as they are}. The exact likelihood then
performs the pre-sample initialisation itself. The truncation is not repaired; it
\textbf{disappears}. With several links into one series, multiply by all of their
denominators, $D_i=\prod_k\delta_k$; each row is built independently, so the network
of \S\ref{sec:network} needs nothing new.

Multiplying the polynomials of \eqref{eq:embed} produces off-diagonal
autoregressive entries that are \emph{products} of structural parameters---for the
factorized numerator $\omega(B)=-x_5(1-x_6B)$ the two entries are $-x_5$ and
$x_5x_6$. Hand-coded implementations of such systems wrote these products directly,
and therefore never had a pre-sample truncation problem to begin with; the slot
table of \S\ref{sec:restrictions} expresses the same products declaratively.

\paragraph{How much does the pre-sample matter?} With both casts available (the
embedded one under the \texttt{-V} option), they can be compared directly. On 200
paired replications of a 69-observation sample with a \emph{rational} transfer (an
infinite impulse response, the least favourable case):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
cast & bias $\omega$ & RMSE $\omega$ & bias $\delta$ & RMSE $\delta$\\
\midrule
subtract & $+0.0017$ & $0.0491$ & $-0.0066$ & $0.0515$\\
embedded & $\mathbf{-0.0002}$ & $0.0490$ & $-0.0057$ & $0.0513$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The bias of $\omega$ falls by a factor of eight, while the RMSE improves by less
than half a percent. The pre-sample matters little for the point estimates. What
the embedded cast secures is not accuracy but \emph{correctness}: the reported
log-likelihood is the exact likelihood of the observed data, so likelihood-ratio
tests and information criteria are valid. Under the subtracting cast the engine
computes an exact likelihood, but of a different series---one whose pre-sample
inputs have been set to zero.

\paragraph{Consistency of the two casts.} The embedded cast is the default; the
subtracting cast is retained under \texttt{-S} for comparison. The test battery
runs \emph{both} casts on every case that distinguishes them---no transfer,
contemporaneous and non-contemporaneous transfers, a rational transfer (which
places a common factor in the autoregressive and moving-average parts), a moving
average, generalized seasonality, a seasonal autoregression, two inputs, the
network, a freed covariance, a shared parameter, and the recursive forecast---and
requires that the parameters, diagnostics, impulse response, gain, forecasts and
variance decomposition agree under either. Making the embedded cast the default
required repairing four points at which the two paths had diverged: the adequacy
diagnostics had to be applied to un-normalized (structural) residuals
$a_{\text{struct}}=\Phi_0\,a_{\text{red}}$ rather than reduced-form ones; the
forecast must not re-add a transfer the VARMA already contains; a freed covariance
must reach the covariance matrix; and the variance decomposition requires the
structural $\psi$ weights $\Psi_{\text{struct}}=\Psi_{\text{red}}\,\Phi_0^{-1}$,
because the reduced-form $\Sigma$ is not diagonal when a transfer is
contemporaneous.

\section{Identification at lag zero}
\label{sec:identification}

A contemporaneous transfer and a freed innovation covariance can describe the same
data, and the two are hard to tell apart in a way that is easy to overlook. This
section states the result; \S\ref{sec:ident-theory} derives it from the reduced
form.

Estimating Spanish CPI $\leftarrow$ WTI with a \emph{contemporaneous} transfer
($b=0$) while also freeing the innovation covariance drives the fit to a corner:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & $b=0$, $\Sigma$ diagonal & $b=0$, $\sigma_{12}$ free \\
\midrule
$\log L$                  & $-718.184$ & $-718.170$ \\
$\omega_0$                & $0.0164$   & $\mathbf{0.1520}$ \\
$\mathrm{corr}(a_N,a_X)$  & $0$        & $\mathbf{-0.985}$ \\
$t$ of $\sigma_{12}$      & ---        & $\mathbf{-2424}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The likelihood does not improve ($\mathrm{LR}=0.029$, against $\chi^2_1 = 3.84$),
yet the point estimates move to extreme values. This is not an optimizer failure
but a nearly flat ridge in the likelihood, and it has an exact explanation.

\paragraph{The prewhitened cross-correlation.} Filter both series by the input's
model: $\alpha_t = \phi_X(B) X_t = a_{X,t}$ and $\beta_t = \phi_X(B) Y_t$.

\begin{description}
\item[(A) Contemporaneous transfer] $Y_t = \omega_0 X_t + N_t$, with $N \perp X$:
\[
\beta_t = \omega_0 a_{X,t} + \phi_X(B) N_t,
\qquad
\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_t,\alpha_{t-k}) =
\begin{cases}
\omega_0\,\sigma_X^2, & k=0,\\[2pt]
0, & k>0 .
\end{cases}
\]
A clean spike at $k=0$.

\item[(B) Innovation covariance] $Y_t = N_t$, with
$\mathrm{cov}(a_{N,t}, a_{X,t}) = \sigma_{12}$:
\[
\beta_t = \phi_X(B)\,\psi_N(B)\,a_{N,t},
\qquad
\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_t,\alpha_{t-k})
 = \sigma_{12}\,\bigl[\,B^k\ \text{coefficient of}\ \phi_X(B)\psi_N(B)\,\bigr].
\]
With AR(1) noise and input this is $\sigma_{12}$ at $k=0$ and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_t,\alpha_{t-k}) \;=\;
\sigma_{12}\;\phi_N^{\,k-1}\,\bigl(\phi_N - \phi_X\bigr),
\qquad k \ge 1 .\;}
\label{eq:tail}
\end{equation}
The \emph{same} spike at $k=0$, plus a tail proportional to
$(\phi_N - \phi_X)$.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Consequence.} If $\phi_N = \phi_X$ the tail in \eqref{eq:tail}
vanishes and the two models are exactly observationally equivalent. This is not
numerical collinearity but non-identification, and the closer the two
autoregressive structures, the weaker the identification. The tail relative to the
spike:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
case & $\phi_N$ & $\phi_X$ & $k=1$ & $k=2$ & $k=3$ \\
\midrule
CPI $\leftarrow$ WTI (real) & $0.403$ & $0.299$ & $\mathbf{+0.104}$ & $+0.042$ & $+0.017$ \\
Synthetic                   & $0.300$ & $0.500$ & $-0.200$ & $-0.060$ & $-0.018$ \\
$\phi_N = \phi_X$           & ---     & ---     & $\mathbf{0.000}$ & $0.000$ & $0.000$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

In the real case, all of the information separating the two mechanisms is a signal
worth $10.4\%$ of the main one. With 215 observations it cannot be resolved, and the
likelihood ridge follows.

\paragraph{Three practical consequences.} Each is verified in the test battery.
\begin{enumerate}
\item With $b \ge 1$ the problem disappears: the transfer contributes nothing at
  $k=0$, so $\sigma_{12}$ is cleanly identified. On synthetic data with $b=2$ and
  a true $\sigma_{12}=0$: $t=1.33$, $p=0.18$, correctly insignificant, with the
  $\omega$'s untouched.
\item \texttt{drtran} warns when a link with $b=0$ has its innovation covariance
  freed at the same time.
\item The two devices are alternatives, not complements: a contemporaneous relation
  is specified \emph{either} through a transfer coefficient \emph{or} through the
  innovation covariance, not both. This is why a well-specified recursive system
  carries off-diagonal innovation covariances and no contemporaneous transfer
  structure, or the reverse, but does not mix them at lag zero.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Characterizing the transfer function: gain and mean lag}
\label{sec:gain}

An estimated transfer function is not described until two quantities are stated:
how much the output responds in total, and how long the response takes. The
$\omega$'s and $\delta$'s are the parametrization, not the response itself.

\begin{align}
\text{gain}\qquad
 g &= \nu(1) \;=\; \frac{\omega(1)}{\delta(1)},
 && \omega(1)=\sum_k \omega_k,\quad \delta(1)=1-\sum_j \delta_j
 \label{eq:gain}\\[4pt]
\text{mean lag}\qquad
 \bar{m} &= \frac{\nu'(1)}{\nu(1)}
   \;=\; b \;+\; \frac{\sum_k k\,\omega_k}{\omega(1)}
             \;+\; \frac{\sum_j j\,\delta_j}{\delta(1)}
 \label{eq:mlag}
\end{align}

The gain is the total, permanent response to a unit, permanent change in the
input: \emph{how much}. The mean lag is the centre of gravity of the response in
time: \emph{how soon}. It is the natural measure of the \emph{speed} of the
relation, and it is what the pure delay $b$ alone cannot tell you---$b$ says when
the response \emph{begins}, not where its mass is. Note in \eqref{eq:mlag} that a
denominator does not change the gain's character but \emph{stretches} the
response: it adds $\sum j\delta_j/\delta(1)$ to the mean lag.

Standard errors follow from the delta method with the covariance matrix of the
free parameters, $\mathrm{var}(f) = \nabla f' \, C \, \nabla f$. The gradient is
assembled in the \emph{free} space, which is where $C$ lives: a fixed slot
contributes nothing, and a shared one contributes to its representative---so the
gain of a transfer whose denominator is shared with the input's own AR gets the
right standard error, and not the one that would follow from treating the two as
independent.

Two checks. First, an exact self-test: with $s=0$ and $r=0$ the gain \emph{is}
$\omega_0$ and the mean lag \emph{is} $b$, so the delta method must reproduce
$\omega_0$'s standard error to the last digit---it does ($0.042634$ in both), with
a mean lag of exactly $1.000000 \pm 0$. Second, against synthetic ground truth:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}

\paragraph{Testing long-run hypotheses.} A unit long-run gain can be imposed by

unrestricted model and comparing likelihoods. With the gain \eqref{eq:gain} and its
standard error, the hypothesis $g=1$ is instead tested directly, on the model
already fitted, with no data transformation and no second estimation.

\section{Example: the pass-through of crude oil to consumer prices}
\label{sec:oil}

The first worked example is bivariate: the effect of the price of crude oil on
consumer price inflation. The exercise illustrates model selection between a
rational and a finite transfer, the gain and mean-lag characterization, and a
forecasting comparison.

\subsection{The transfer is not rational}

Three fits, same output (Spanish CPI) and same input (WTI crude), the first two
with exactly the same number of free parameters:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrl}
\toprule
model & free & $\log L$ & adequacy \\
\midrule
$\omega_0/(1-\delta_1 B)$ \quad (rational)      & 17 & $-721.72$ & \textbf{INADEQUATE} ($p=0.033$) \\
$\omega_0 + \omega_1 B$ \quad (two $\omega$'s)  & 17 & $\mathbf{-718.18}$ & adequate ($p=0.196$) \\
$(\omega_0+\omega_1 B)/(1-\delta_1 B)$ \quad (nests the above) & 18 & $-718.18$ & adequate \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The rational form \emph{loses with the same number of parameters}. The third fit,
which nests the second, settles it: $\delta_1 = -0.010$ with $t=-0.055$;
$\mathrm{LR}=0.003$ against $\chi^2_1=3.84$. There is no denominator.

The reason is visible in the impulse weights:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule

\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The data want $\nu_1/\nu_0 \approx 0.66$ \emph{and} $\nu_2 = 0$. A geometric decay
cannot do both. The adequacy test catches it exactly there: the noise still
carries a trace of the input at $k=2$, with $r=-0.14$---negative, because the
rational form puts \emph{too much} weight at that lag.

\paragraph{A methodological warning.} In the forced rational fit, $\delta_1 =
0.458$ with $t=6.40$ appears decisive, but the significance is misleading. With the
denominator imposed, $\delta$ is the only route to any weight at lag one, so its
significance says ``there is a response at lag one'', not ``the response is
geometric''. The valid test is the one in the nesting model, where $\delta_1$ is
insignificant.

Characterized by \eqref{eq:gain}--\eqref{eq:mlag}, the relation is
\[
g = 0.027195 \;(0.002453),\qquad
\bar{m} = 0.397 \;(0.044)\ \text{months},\qquad b=0 .
\]
The pass-through of crude to the Spanish CPI is \emph{immediate}---its centre of
gravity is less than half a month---and exhausted within two; it is not a
distributed lag that propagates. The estimate $\omega = (0.0164,\,0.0108)$
reproduces the previously documented values $(0.0154,\,0.0104)$.


\subsection{Does the multivariate model forecast better?}
\label{sec:passthrough}

A natural question is whether adding the oil price improves the inflation forecast.
A common way to ask it is a bivariate VAR of inflation and the oil price, estimated
for several countries (here Spain, France and Germany). The VAR, however, is
inadequate for a structural reason. It imposes the same lag structure on every
equation, with full coefficient matrices, and the consumer-price series do not
admit that: their univariate models, identified independently, are Spain AR(1)
plus harmonics; France a seasonal AR$(1)_{12}$; Germany AR(3) plus a seasonal
AR$(1)_{12}$ plus harmonics. A regular VAR$(p)$ with harmonic deseasonalization
cannot carry these, and the residual portmanteau fails for France ($p=0.006$) and
Germany ($p=0.018$). Raising $p$ to absorb the seasonality would introduce
off-diagonal terms at high lags, and with them a spurious feedback from inflation
to the oil price.

The transfer function cast avoids this. Each series keeps its own univariate model
because the VARMA is diagonal, and the transfer is unidirectional by construction,
so there is no lag order to raise and no feedback to invent. Estimating each CPI
against WTI with $b=0$, $r=0$, $s=1$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrl}
\toprule

\midrule
Spain   & $0.0272$ & $(0.0025)$ & $0.40$ & passes ($p=0.46$) \\
France  & $0.0155$ & $(0.0015)$ & $0.43$ & \textbf{passes} ($p=0.20$); the VAR failed ($0.006$) \\
Germany & $0.0127$ & $(0.0021)$ & $0.14$ & marginal ($p=0.03$); the VAR failed ($0.018$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The long-run elasticities order as one would expect (Spain $2.7\%$, France
$1.35\%$, Germany $1.1\%$), and in Germany the lagged coefficient is insignificant
($t=0.97$): the German channel is essentially contemporaneous. France's noise now
passes the adequacy test where the VAR failed; Germany's improves but remains
marginal, its univariate model being the most demanding of the three and its
relation the weakest.

\paragraph{A caveat on theoretical variances.} These are theoretical forecast
error variances, computed from the fitted models. They report what the models
imply, not what happens out of sample, where parameter uncertainty and structural
change---the 2021--22 inflation surge falls in this sample---also act. A genuine
out-of-sample comparison requires recursive forecasting with the parameters held
fixed; it is the subject of \S\ref{sec:recursive}, and its conclusion differs from
what the theoretical variances suggest. The mechanism above remains valid---the oil
price's own unpredictability is large, and beyond one step the transfer imports a
substantial share of the variance of the oil forecast---but it does not follow that
the transfer forecasts worse.

A model's forecast variance is computed under the assumption that the model is

\subsection{The reduced-form covariance is not a free parameter}
\label{sec:embedcov}

The embedded cast does not require estimating an innovation covariance matrix, and
the reason is a direct confirmation of \S\ref{sec:identification}. The structural
covariance stays diagonal: one innovation per series, uncorrelated. When the
transfer is contemporaneous ($b=0$),
$\omega_0$ lands at lag zero, so $\Phi_0 \ne I$ and the system must be normalized by
$\Phi_0^{-1}$. With $m=2$,
\[
\Phi_0=\begin{pmatrix}1 & -\omega_0\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
Q_{\text{red}}=\Phi_0^{-1}Q\,\Phi_0^{-\top}
=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_N^2+\omega_0^2\sigma_X^2 & \omega_0\sigma_X^2\\[2pt]
                \omega_0\sigma_X^2 & \sigma_X^2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The reduced-form covariance is $\sigma_{12}=\omega_0\sigma_X^2$: a \emph{function}
of $\omega_0$, not a new parameter. Verified on Spanish CPI against WTI:
$\omega_0\,\Sigma_{22}=0.016400\times 68.787225=1.128110$, against the
$\Sigma_{12}=1.128097$ the program reports. With $b=1$ the covariance is exactly
zero, and the model has one free parameter fewer---the one belonging to $\omega_1$,
not to any covariance.

This is the identity of a structural VAR: \textbf{a contemporaneous structural
coefficient \emph{generates} the reduced-form covariance.} It is why the two cannot
both be estimated (\S\ref{sec:identification}).

\paragraph{The same result from the reduced form.} After normalizing, the
off-diagonal autoregressive coefficient at lag one is
\[
\bigl[\Phi_0^{-1}\Phi_1\bigr]_{12}
 \;=\; -\omega_0\phi_N + \omega_0\phi_X
 \;=\; \boxed{\;\omega_0\,(\phi_X-\phi_N)\;}\,,
\]
the only trace left, once the system is written in reduced form, that the relation
is a transfer rather than an innovation covariance. It vanishes when
$\phi_N=\phi_X$---the condition of \S\ref{sec:identification}, reached here by a
different route. On the real data it evaluates to $0.0164\times 0.0040 = 0.000065$,
negligible for practical purposes: the Spanish contemporaneous transfer model is, to
a good approximation, a diagonal model with a correlated innovation.


\section{The transfer function as a restricted VARMA}
\label{sec:theory}

This section states the transformation formally. The algebra is a one-line
manipulation, but the remarks that follow it govern the implementation and are
recorded in full. The device---reducing a structured model to a plain VARMA to which
a direct exact likelihood then applies unchanged---is that of
Mauricio~\cite{mauricio2006}, where it is used to estimate partially nonstationary
vector ARMA models by transforming the error-correction representation into a
standard, stationary VARMA. What follows applies the same device to a transfer
function.

\subsection{Setup}

Let $w_{1t}$ be an output and $w_{2t},\dots,w_{mt}$ be $k=m-1$ inputs, all
stationary (after the Box--Cox transformation, the differencing and the removal of
the deterministic terms that each series' univariate specification prescribes).
Write the transfer function model as
\begin{align}
w_{1t} &= \sum_{j=1}^{k}\nu_j(B)\,w_{j+1,t} + N_t,
&& \nu_j(B)=\frac{\omega_j(B)}{\delta_j(B)}B^{b_j},
\label{eq:tfm}\\
\phi_1(B)\,N_t &= \theta_1(B)\,a_{1t},
&& \phi_{j+1}(B)\,w_{j+1,t}=\theta_{j+1}(B)\,a_{j+1,t},
\label{eq:arma}
\end{align}
with $\omega_j(B)=\omega_{j0}+\dots+\omega_{js_j}B^{s_j}$, $\delta_j(0)=1$, and
\begin{equation}
a_t=(a_{1t},\dots,a_{mt})' \sim \mathrm{iid}\; N(0,\sigma^2 Q),
\qquad Q \ \text{diagonal}.
\label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:Q} is the model's substantive assumption: the noise innovation and
the input innovations are \emph{orthogonal}. It is what ``the input is exogenous''
means here, and it is testable (\S\ref{sec:identification}).

Write $D(B)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\delta_j(B)$ and $D_{-j}(B)=D(B)/\delta_j(B)$.

\subsection{The transformation}

\begin{proposition}[The cast]
\label{prop:cast}
Let $T(B)=\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\phi_1(B)D(B),\,1,\dots,1\bigr)$ act on
\eqref{eq:tfm}--\eqref{eq:arma}. Then $w_t=(w_{1t},\dots,w_{mt})'$ satisfies the
VARMA
\begin{equation}
P(B)\,w_t = M(B)\,a_t,
\label{eq:varma}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
P_{11}(B) &= \phi_1(B)\,D(B), &
P_{1,j+1}(B) &= -\,\phi_1(B)\,\omega_j(B)\,B^{b_j}\,D_{-j}(B),\notag\\
P_{ii}(B) &= \phi_i(B)\ (i\ge 2), &
P_{i\ell}(B) &= 0 \ \ \text{otherwise},
\label{eq:P}\\
M_{11}(B) &= D(B)\,\theta_1(B), &
M_{ii}(B) &= \theta_i(B)\ (i\ge 2),
\label{eq:M}
\end{align}
and $M_{i\ell}=0$ off the diagonal. The innovation covariance is unchanged:
$\sigma^2 Q$, diagonal.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Multiply \eqref{eq:tfm} by $\phi_1(B)D(B)$. On the left, $\phi_1 D\,w_{1t}$. On the
right, $\phi_1 D\,\nu_j(B) w_{j+1,t} = \phi_1\,\omega_j B^{b_j} D_{-j}\,w_{j+1,t}$
because $D/\delta_j=D_{-j}$; and $\phi_1 D\,N_t = D\,\theta_1 a_{1t}$
by~\eqref{eq:arma}. The remaining rows are \eqref{eq:arma} unchanged.
\end{proof}

Only the first row couples the series; rows $2,\dots,m$ are the inputs' own
univariate models. So $P(B)$ is triangular in the sense that matters:

\begin{corollary}[Stationarity and invertibility]
\label{cor:stat}
$|P(B)| = \phi_1(B)\,D(B)\prod_{i\ge2}\phi_i(B)$ and
$|M(B)| = D(B)\,\theta_1(B)\prod_{i\ge2}\theta_i(B)$. Hence the VARMA
\eqref{eq:varma} is stationary if and only if the noise and every input are
stationary \emph{and} every transfer denominator $\delta_j(B)$ is stable; and it is
invertible if and only if every $\theta_i(B)$ is invertible \emph{and}, again, every
$\delta_j(B)$ is stable.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[The cast is a non-minimal realisation]
\label{rem:common}
$D(B)$ divides \emph{both} $|P(B)|$ and $|M(B)|$. The representation therefore
carries a common factor: its McMillan degree exceeds that of the underlying process.
This is harmless for identification---the coefficients of $\delta_j$ are constrained
to be the \emph{same} polynomial in the autoregressive and the moving-average part,
so the map from structural parameters to \eqref{eq:P}--\eqref{eq:M} is
injective---but it is not harmless numerically: an almost-cancelling factor is a
delicate object. It is the reason the optimiser needs more iterations as
$\delta \to 1$ (\S\ref{sec:mcstudy}), and it is what one pays for the exactness.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Normalisation: why \texorpdfstring{$\Phi(0)=I$}{Phi(0)=I} is not optional}

The likelihood algorithm requires the model in the form
$\Phi(B)\tilde w_t=\Theta(B)a_t$ with $\Phi(B)=I-\Phi_1B-\dots$ and
$\Theta(B)=I-\Theta_1B-\dots$, that is, with $\Phi(0)=\Theta(0)=I$
\cite{mauricio1997}. The cast does not automatically deliver that.

\begin{proposition}[Normalisation]
\label{prop:norm}
Let $\Lambda$ be the $m\times m$ matrix whose only nonzero entries are
$\Lambda_{1,j+1}=\omega_{j0}$ for those inputs with $b_j=0$. Then
$P(0)=I-\Lambda$, and since $\Lambda^2=0$,
\begin{equation}
P(0)^{-1} = I+\Lambda .
\label{eq:P0inv}
\end{equation}
Premultiplying \eqref{eq:varma} by $P(0)^{-1}$ gives a VARMA in the required form,
with
\begin{equation}
\Phi_k = P(0)^{-1}P_k,\qquad
\Theta_k = P(0)^{-1}M_k\,,\qquad
\Sigma_{\mathrm{red}} = \sigma^2\,(I+\Lambda)\,Q\,(I+\Lambda)' .
\label{eq:normal}
\end{equation}
If no transfer is contemporaneous ($b_j\ge 1$ for all $j$), then $\Lambda=0$ and
nothing happens.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$P_{11}(0)=\phi_1(0)D(0)=1$ and $P_{ii}(0)=1$; the off-diagonal
$P_{1,j+1}(0)=-\phi_1(0)\omega_j(0)\,[\,b_j=0\,]=-\omega_{j0}[\,b_j=0\,]$. So
$P(0)=I-\Lambda$. $\Lambda$ has nonzero entries only in row~1, and its first column
is zero, so $\Lambda^2=0$ and $(I-\Lambda)^{-1}=I+\Lambda$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The reduced-form covariance is not a parameter]
\label{cor:sigma}
Writing $\sigma^2 Q=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1^2,\dots,\sigma_m^2)$,
\eqref{eq:normal} gives
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_{\mathrm{red}} =
\begin{pmatrix}
\sigma_1^2+\sum_j \omega_{j0}^2\sigma_{j+1}^2 & \cdots & \omega_{j0}\sigma_{j+1}^2 & \cdots\\
\vdots & & &\\
\omega_{j0}\sigma_{j+1}^2 & & \sigma_{j+1}^2 &\\
\vdots & & &
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
so the innovations of the \emph{observed} series are correlated whenever a transfer
is contemporaneous, with $\Sigma_{1,j+1}=\omega_{j0}\,\Sigma_{j+1,j+1}$. This is a
\textbf{function of $\omega_{j0}$, not a free parameter}: the degrees of freedom are
unchanged. A contemporaneous structural coefficient \emph{generates} the reduced-form
covariance. It is the identity of a structural VAR, and it is why the two cannot both
be estimated (\S\ref{sec:identification}).
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[The residuals, and what the un-normalisation is]
\label{cor:chol}
The likelihood returns the reduced-form innovations $u_t = P(0)^{-1}a_t$. The
structural ones are recovered exactly, with no choice to make:
\begin{equation}
a_t = P(0)\,u_t = (I-\Lambda)\,u_t .
\end{equation}
Moreover $P(0)^{-1}=I+\Lambda$ is unit triangular, so \emph{this un-normalisation is
a Cholesky factorisation of $\Sigma_{\mathrm{red}}$ with the inputs ordered first.}
The transfer model with a contemporaneous term is therefore a recursive (Wold) system
identified by a Cholesky ordering---but the ordering is not the analyst's choice: it
is the exogeneity assumption \eqref{eq:Q}, which is declared and \emph{tested}.
\end{corollary}

Corollary~\ref{cor:chol} is where the honest answer to ``is this better than a VAR''
lives (\S\ref{sec:irf}). It is not better because it escapes the ordering. It is
better because the ordering is a hypothesis rather than a convention. And with
$b_j\ge1$ for all $j$ the question does not arise at all: $\Lambda=0$, the
innovations are already orthogonal, and there is nothing to order. \textbf{The entire
identification problem lives at lag zero.}

\subsection{Exactness of the pre-sample}

\begin{proposition}[Exactness]
\label{prop:exact}
Let $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}$ be the likelihood of \eqref{eq:varma} evaluated on
$w=(w_1,\dots,w_m)$, and let $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sub}}$ be the likelihood obtained
by first forming $\hat N_t = w_{1t}-\sum_j\sum_{k=1}^{t}\nu_{jk}\,w_{j+1,t-k+1}$ and
then evaluating the diagonal VARMA on $(\hat N,w_2,\dots,w_m)$. Then
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}$ is the exact likelihood of the data;
\item $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sub}}$ is the exact likelihood of a \emph{different}
      vector, namely one in which the pre-sample inputs have been set to zero;
\item the two coincide identically if and only if $\nu_{jk}=0$ for all $k\ge2$ and
      all $j$---that is, if and only if every transfer is purely contemporaneous
      ($b_j=s_j=r_j=0$).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) The map $(w_1,\dots,w_m)\mapsto$ \eqref{eq:varma} is the model itself; no data
are altered. (ii) $\hat N_t$ differs from $N_t$ by
$e_t=\sum_j\sum_{k>t}\nu_{jk}w_{j+1,t-k+1}$, the omitted pre-sample terms; the
Jacobian of $(w_1,w_2,\dots)\mapsto(\hat N,w_2,\dots)$ is unity, so
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sub}}$ is a proper likelihood---of the contaminated vector.
(iii) $e_t\equiv0$ for all $t\ge1$ iff $\nu_{jk}=0$ for $k\ge2$.
\end{proof}

Part (iii) is a sharp, falsifiable prediction, and the program obeys it: with
$b=r=s=0$ the two casts return the same log-likelihood to the last bit
($-833.415278$, difference $0.0\mathrm{e}{+}00$), and they differ as soon as $b\ge1$
or $s\ge1$. It also says exactly \emph{when} the old cast is harmless, and exactly
when it is not: the damage is carried by the tail of $\nu(B)$, so it is negligible
for a finite transfer and grows with the memory of a rational one. That is what
\S\ref{sec:mcstudy} measures.

\begin{remark}[Why the input is modelled, and not conditioned on]
\label{rem:endog}
The pre-sample problem has a published solution: put the model in state space and
treat the initial states of the rational filter as diffuse, estimating them
\cite[\S3.2.1, field \texttt{ser.inc}]{gomez2019}---the alternative there being to
set them to zero, which is precisely the truncation. That route keeps the input
\emph{strongly exogenous}: given, not modelled, so its pre-sample must be estimated.

The route taken here is different. The input is a \emph{series of the system}, with
its own ARMA \eqref{eq:arma}; its pre-sample is therefore governed by the stationary
distribution of \eqref{eq:varma} and is integrated out by the exact likelihood, at no
cost in parameters. In a Box--Jenkins analysis this is free: the input's univariate
model is already there, because one needs it to prewhiten and to forecast.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Observational equivalence in the reduced form}
\label{sec:ident-theory}

\begin{proposition}[Observational equivalence]
\label{prop:ident}
Take $m=2$, one contemporaneous transfer ($b=r=s=0$, coefficient $\omega_0$), AR(1)
noise $\phi_N$ and AR(1) input $\phi_X$. The normalised reduced form is a VAR(1) with
\begin{equation}
\Phi_1 = P(0)^{-1}P_1
       = \begin{pmatrix}\phi_N & \omega_0(\phi_X-\phi_N)\\[2pt] 0 & \phi_X\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Sigma_{\mathrm{red}}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_N^2+\omega_0^2\sigma_X^2 & \omega_0\sigma_X^2\\[2pt]
\omega_0\sigma_X^2 & \sigma_X^2\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:ident}
\end{equation}
Consequently, if $\phi_N=\phi_X$ the off-diagonal autoregressive coefficient
vanishes and the model is \emph{identical} to a diagonal VAR(1) with correlated
innovations---that is, to a model with \emph{no} transfer and a free innovation
covariance. The two mechanisms are then observationally equivalent, and no test can
prefer one.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$P_{11}(B)=1-\phi_NB$, $P_{12}(B)=-\omega_0(1-\phi_NB)$, $P_{22}(B)=1-\phi_XB$;
so $P_1=\begin{psmallmatrix}\phi_N & -\omega_0\phi_N\\ 0 & \phi_X\end{psmallmatrix}$
and $P(0)^{-1}=\begin{psmallmatrix}1&\omega_0\\0&1\end{psmallmatrix}$ by
\eqref{eq:P0inv}. Multiply. $\Sigma_{\mathrm{red}}$ is
Corollary~\ref{cor:sigma}.
\end{proof}

The single quantity $\omega_0(\phi_X-\phi_N)$ is therefore \emph{the entire trace},
in reduced form, that the relation is a \emph{transfer} rather than a mere innovation
covariance. It is not only a vanishing condition: it is a \emph{measure of how much
information there is} to tell the two apart. On the Spanish CPI against WTI it
evaluates to $0.0164\times0.0040=0.000065$. Zero, for all practical purposes.

Verified on constructed truth (both models fitted with the same number of free
parameters; the truth is a contemporaneous transfer with independent innovations):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccrr}
\toprule
 & $\phi_N$ & $\phi_X$ & $\log L$ transfer & $\log L$ covariance \\
\midrule
non-identified & $0.5$ & $0.5$ & $-833.456409$ & $-833.456409$ \\
separable      & $0.2$ & $0.7$ & $-847.854128$ & $-874.298278$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Identical to the last digit when $\phi_N=\phi_X$; separated by 26 points of
log-likelihood when the two autoregressions differ. Identification arises precisely
from that difference.

\paragraph{The practical consequence.} For the Spanish pass-through the sample
cannot determine whether the oil price acts through a contemporaneous transfer or
through an innovation covariance. What decides is not the data but the exogeneity of
the oil price---which is tested, and passes---together with the economic reading.
The transfer is then the natural causal interpretation, but it is an interpretation
the model assumes. Stating this explicitly locates the empirical content
(exogeneity, the gain of $2.7\%$, the out-of-sample improvement) and separates it
from the identifying assumption.

\subsection{Relation to existing results}
\label{sec:novelty}

Nothing above is a new statistical result, and it is worth being precise about why.

\begin{itemize}
\item That a transfer function model is a restricted VARMAX is standard
  \cite{lutkepohl2005, reinsel1997, gomez2019}.
\item Exact maximum likelihood for such models is solved, by state space and the
  Kalman filter with a properly initialised state \cite{gomez2019, harvey1989}. The
  pre-sample of the rational filter is handled there by estimating its initial
  states.
\item Reducing a structured model to a \emph{plain} VARMA so that a direct exact
  likelihood applies unchanged is Mauricio's own device, used in \cite{mauricio2006}
  for the error-correction representation.
\item The direct (non-Kalman) exact VARMA likelihood is
  \cite{mauricio1995,mauricio1997,mauricio2002}, with \cite{shea1989} the
  alternative.
\end{itemize}

The particular combination used here---reducing \eqref{eq:tfm} to \eqref{eq:varma}
by premultiplication, estimating it with a \emph{direct} exact VARMA likelihood, and
modelling the input \emph{inside the system} so that its pre-sample is integrated out
rather than estimated (Remark~\ref{rem:endog})---is a straightforward assembly of
these ingredients; the algebra is a one-line manipulation. What is worth recording is
the set of properties on which a correct implementation depends:
Remark~\ref{rem:common}, Proposition~\ref{prop:norm},
Corollaries~\ref{cor:sigma} and~\ref{cor:chol}, and Proposition~\ref{prop:ident}.
Each is elementary, and each has a direct implementation consequence.


\section{How much does exactness buy? A Monte Carlo study}
\label{sec:mcstudy}

Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} says the subtracting cast conditions on pre-sample
inputs equal to zero, and that the two casts coincide only for a purely
contemporaneous transfer. It does not say how much that matters. It should matter
most where the impulse response is \emph{long}, because the omitted terms are its
tail: for $\nu(B)=\omega B^{b}/(1-\delta B)$ the truncation error at time $t$ decays
like $\delta^{t}$, so the contaminated stretch grows with $\delta$ and dilutes with
$n$. With $\delta=0.85$ and $n=69$---the length of the \texttt{m6} systems---some
twenty of the sixty-nine observations carry a truncation error above $10\%$ of the
noise's standard deviation; with $\delta=0.30$, three.

\paragraph{Efficacy.} The advantage behaves exactly as the truncation error does. It
is nil at $\delta=0.30$, appears at $0.60$, and becomes large at $0.85$ and beyond;
and it dilutes with the sample---at $n=400$ and $\delta=0.85$ it has essentially
vanished. But at $\delta=0.95$ it survives even $n=400$, because a memory that long
contaminates a long stretch of any sample: the RMSE of the gain falls by $60\%$.

Note \emph{where} the damage sits. It is not in $\omega$, which is a local weight,
but in $\delta$, which governs the \emph{tail}---and the tail is precisely what
truncation destroys. That is why the gain $\nu(1)=\omega/(1-\delta)$, which depends
on $\delta$ through a small denominator, suffers most of all. Put plainly:
\textbf{the subtracting cast cannot estimate a long memory well, because it has
erased the memory}.

\paragraph{Efficiency.} For short samples the embedded cast costs $15$--$27\%$ more
time, and needs more iterations as $\delta$ grows ($14\to39$). This is
Remark~\ref{rem:common} made visible: the cast carries the common factor $D(B)$ in
both $|P|$ and $|M|$, and an almost-cancelling factor is a delicate object for an
optimizer. It is the price of the exactness.

At $n=400$, however, the embedded cast is \emph{twice as fast}. This is a matter of
complexity, not of statistics. The subtracting cast forms the transfer by
convolution,
\[
\text{transfer}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\nu_k\,w_{X,t-k+1},\qquad t=1,\dots,n,
\]
which is $O(n^2)$ \emph{per objective evaluation, per link}. The embedded cast
convolves nothing: it multiplies a few short polynomials---$O(1)$ in $n$---and hands
the raw series to the likelihood. So the subtracting cast is quadratic in the sample
and the embedded one is linear, and they cross. At $n=69$ the higher VARMA orders
dominate and the embedded cast is slower; by $n=400$ the $O(n^2)$ convolution
dominates and it is $2\times$ faster.

\paragraph{Convergence.} Both casts converge in every replication except at
$\delta=0.95$, where the embedded cast converges in $85$--$88\%$ of them. At a memory
that close to a unit root the common factor of Remark~\ref{rem:common} is a
near-exact cancellation and the likelihood surface is ill-conditioned. This is a
genuine limitation of the exact cast, recorded in \S\ref{sec:open}.

\paragraph{A robustness fix uncovered by the study.} At $\delta\approx0.95$ the
optimizer can reach a region where the objective returns \texttt{NaN}. The line
search backtracks by interpolating on the function value: a \texttt{NaN} objective
yields a \texttt{NaN} step length, and then \texttt{lambda < minlam} is false---every
comparison with \texttt{NaN} is---so the step is neither accepted nor abandoned, and
the search does not terminate. No iteration limit guards against this, since the loop
is inside a single iteration. The remedy is the one prescribed for inadmissible
points~\cite{mauricio1997}: set the scaled objective to one. A point that yields a
non-finite likelihood is inadmissible and is now rejected as such.

\section{Impulse response and variance decomposition}
\label{sec:irf}

The weights $\nu_k$ are the impulse response, which is one convenience of the
transfer function form. Two further quantities make them comparable across models,
and both are reported.

\paragraph{The quantified impulse response.} Each $\nu_k$ with its standard error,
and the \emph{cumulative} response, which is the reply to a \emph{permanent} unit
change and converges to the gain. Both standard errors come from the delta method,
with the gradients obtained from the same recursion that generates the weights:
\[
\nu_t=\omega_{t-b}+\sum_j \delta_j\nu_{t-j}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\frac{\partial \nu_t}{\partial \omega_i}=\mathbb{1}[t-b=i]+\sum_j\delta_j\frac{\partial \nu_{t-j}}{\partial\omega_i},
\quad
\frac{\partial \nu_t}{\partial \delta_i}=\nu_{t-i}+\sum_j\delta_j\frac{\partial \nu_{t-j}}{\partial\delta_i}.
\]
A self-test: when the transfer is a single weight, $\mathrm{SE}(\nu_b)$ must equal
$\mathrm{SE}(\omega_0)$ to the last digit. It does.

\paragraph{The forecast error variance decomposition.} With $\Sigma$ diagonal,
\[
\text{share}_{ij}(l) \;=\; \frac{\sigma_{jj}\sum_{t<l}\Psi^{\text{level}}_{ij}(t)^2}{V_i(l)},
\]
clean and unique, because the innovations are orthogonal and there is nothing to
order. For Spanish CPI against WTI:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{crr}
\toprule
$l$ & own noise & \textbf{WTI} \\
\midrule
 1 & $68.2\%$ & $\mathbf{31.8\%}$ \\
 3 & $49.9\%$ & $\mathbf{50.1\%}$ \\
12 & $44.7\%$ & $\mathbf{55.3\%}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A third of the one-month error, and \emph{half} by three months, is the oil
innovation. Note that this is the same fact as \S\ref{sec:passthrough} seen from
the other side: the oil price contributes a great deal of \emph{variance}. What
\S\ref{sec:recursive} added is that it nevertheless contributes enough
\emph{signal} to be worth it---in Spain.

\paragraph{Comparison with a VAR.} In a VAR the impulse response is not identified
without an ordering, because $\Sigma$ is not diagonal: one variable must be shocked
first, and the response changes with that choice. In the transfer function the
identification is part of the model specification---$\nu(B)$ is estimated directly
and $Q$ is diagonal. This does not escape the identification problem; it relocates
the restrictions so that they are visible:

\begin{itemize}
\item the restrictions---an exogenous input, a diagonal $\Sigma$---are declared, not
  encoded in an ordering;
\item they are testable: the cross-correlation at negative lags is an exogeneity
  test, and the adequacy test asks whether $\nu(B)$ is wide enough;
\item they can be relaxed, one covariance at a time; and
\item when a covariance is relaxed next to a contemporaneous transfer, the program
  returns to exactly the VAR's identification problem---$\omega_0$ and $\sigma_{12}$
  explain the same thing at lag zero (\S\ref{sec:identification})---and reports the
  non-identification rather than a spurious estimate.
\end{itemize}

Accordingly, the variance decomposition is not reported when $\Sigma$ is
non-diagonal: with correlated innovations the decomposition is not unique, and
assigning the common part requires a Cholesky ordering---the VAR's problem, avoided
while $\Sigma$ stays diagonal and declared when it does not. The advantage over a VAR
is therefore modest and of a specific kind: not fewer assumptions, but assumptions
that are explicit, testable and refusable, together with the practical advantage that
each series may keep the univariate model it requires (\S\ref{sec:passthrough}).

\section{Example: a recursive model of employment by sector}
\label{sec:m6}

The second worked example exercises the parts of the program that the bivariate
pass-through does not: a network of transfers, generalized seasonality, factorized
numerators with shared parameters, and the guided identification of the system. The
data and model are those of a multivariate study of Spanish employment by
sector~\cite{relloso1997}: six quarterly series over 1976:III--1993:II (69
observations)---the active population (\texttt{P}), unemployment (\texttt{EP}), and
employment in agriculture (\texttt{EA}), industry (\texttt{EI}), construction
(\texttt{EU}) and services (\texttt{EC}).

\subsection{The system}

The six series form a recursive dynamic system: a directed acyclic graph of
transfers with a chain through the productive sectors into unemployment, plus a
direct link,
\begin{center}
\texttt{EC} $\xrightarrow{\,b=2\,}$ \texttt{EU} $\xrightarrow{\,b=1\,}$
\texttt{EI} $\xrightarrow{\,b=1\,}$ \texttt{EP}\,,
\qquad
\texttt{EC} $\xrightarrow{\,b=1\,}$ \texttt{EP}\,.
\end{center}
Each series carries its own univariate model. Five are twice-differenced ($\nabla^2$)
with deterministic seasonality and intervention terms; agricultural employment
\texttt{EA} carries \emph{generalized} (stochastic) seasonality---a
$\nabla\nabla_4$ difference with a moving-average factor at each seasonal frequency,
$\theta=0.43$ at frequency zero and $\lambda_1=-0.68$, $\lambda_2=-0.72$ at the
harmonic and Nyquist frequencies. Because the VARMA cast keeps $\Phi$, $\Theta$ and
$Q$ diagonal, each of these univariate specifications survives unchanged inside the
system, exactly as in the pass-through example.

\subsection{Identification of the network}

The system is built in the order of the classical ladder. First the six univariate
models are estimated jointly as a diagonal system (\texttt{-0}); this reproduces the
univariate fits and yields the log-likelihood of the diagonal baseline,
$-1709.51$. Its residual cross-correlations are then read to propose the transfer
links and the contemporaneous covariances (\texttt{-i}, or \texttt{-g} to write the
proposal directly to files). On these data the procedure recovers three
contemporaneous covariances---$\mathrm{cov}(\texttt{EA},\texttt{EI})$,
$\mathrm{cov}(\texttt{EA},\texttt{EC})$, $\mathrm{cov}(\texttt{EI},\texttt{EU})$---and
the clean directed link $\texttt{EI}\rightarrow\texttt{EP}$ at lag~1. The proposed
network is a guide to be pruned by exogeneity, acyclicity and lag plausibility, not a
final model.

Estimating the pruned network with free transfer numerators raises the
log-likelihood to $-1697.61$, a gain of about twelve points over the diagonal for
the added transfer parameters.

\subsection{Factorized numerators}

The distinctive feature of this system is that its transfer numerators are
\emph{factorized}, and two of them share a parameter with the moving-average
operator of their own input---the signature of a transfer that inherits part of the
dynamics of its input. In the Box--Jenkins convention $\omega(B)=\omega_0-\omega_1B$,
a numerator $-\omega_0(1-mB)$ has coefficient $\omega_1=\omega_0\,m$, so a shared
factor $m$ is expressed as a product:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
link & factorized numerator & slot-table restriction \\
\midrule
$\texttt{EP}\leftarrow\texttt{EI}$ & $-\omega_0(1-m_{\texttt{EI}}B)$
   & \texttt{omega1[1] = omega1[0] * theta\_2} \\
$\texttt{EU}\leftarrow\texttt{EC}$ & $-\omega_0(1-m_{\texttt{EU}}B)$
   & \texttt{omega4[1] = omega4[0] * theta\_3} \\
$\texttt{EI}\leftarrow\texttt{EU}$ & $-(1-B)(x_0+x_1B+x_2B^2)$
   & \texttt{omega3[0] = omega3[1] + omega3[2] + omega3[3]} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The first two are products; \texttt{theta\_2} and \texttt{theta\_3} are the
moving-average parameters of \texttt{EI} and \texttt{EU}, shared into their
respective transfers. The third numerator carries a \emph{fixed} factor $(1-B)$,
which forces the impulse-response numerator to vanish at $B=1$; in the Box--Jenkins
convention this is the linear constraint
$\omega_0=\omega_1+\omega_2+\omega_3$, a linear combination of slots. These are
exactly the product and linear-combination forms of the slot table
(\S\ref{sec:restrictions}). A fourth link, $\texttt{EP}\leftarrow\texttt{EC}$, has a
numerator $(1-x_2B)(x_0+x_1B)$ whose three parameters are not shared with any other
operator; a free quadratic numerator spans the same space, so no restriction is
imposed.

\subsection{The restrictions are consistent with the data}

Because the compound end-of-sample interventions of this system are weakly
identified, and can drift between modes in the reduced network, the effect of the
transfer restrictions is isolated by holding the deterministic terms fixed at their
univariate values (\texttt{-D -E}). Against that reference:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
transfer numerators & free params & $\log L$ & restriction \\
\midrule
free                                   & 27 & $-1728.91$ & --- \\
two shared-MA products                 & 25 & $-1729.31$ & $\Delta=0.4$, 2 d.f. \\
$+$ the fixed $(1-B)$ factor           & 24 & $-1731.40$ & $\Delta=2.5$, 3 d.f. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Imposing the full factorized structure of the system costs $2.5$ points of
log-likelihood over three degrees of freedom; the likelihood-ratio statistic is
$5.0$, against $\chi^2_{3,0.95}=7.81$. The restrictions are not rejected: the
parameter sharing that the factorized form encodes is consistent with the data, and
it moves the estimated coefficients toward their factorized values at negligible cost
in fit. The example thus exercises the network cast, generalized seasonality, and
both the product and linear-combination restrictions in a single system, and shows
that the system's classical structure survives an exact-likelihood re-estimation.

\section{Scope, generalization, and the model's place}
\label{sec:scope}

\subsection{Generic cases the program is intended for}

The program estimates, by exact maximum likelihood, any model that can be written as
a system of the form \eqref{eq:tfm}--\eqref{eq:arma}: a collection of time series in
which

\begin{itemize}
\item each series has its own univariate ARIMA or generalized-seasonal model, with
  deterministic components (calendar effects, interventions, a mean);
\item some series are related by \emph{one-directional} rational transfer functions
  $\nu(B)=\omega(B)/\delta(B)\,B^b$, possibly forming a directed acyclic graph in
  which a series is at once a target and a source; and
\item the innovations across series are orthogonal, except for contemporaneous
  covariances that are explicitly freed.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
This covers single-equation regression with ARMA errors and lagged inputs (a single
transfer, no covariances); the classical single-output transfer function model with
several exogenous inputs; intervention analysis (a transfer whose input is a
deterministic indicator); and recursive multivariate systems such as the employment
example of \S\ref{sec:m6}. It does \emph{not} cover feedback systems, in which two
series each Granger-cause the other: a cycle cannot be triangularized by subtraction,
and the program rejects it. Genuine simultaneity requires the full VARMA, of which
this model is a restriction.

\subsection{Of what the model is a particular case}

The transfer function system is a \emph{restricted VARMA}. Proposition~\ref{prop:cast}
exhibits the restriction: a VARMA $P(B)w_t=M(B)a_t$ whose autoregressive matrix
$P(B)$ is lower-Hessenberg with the coupling confined to the output equation, whose
moving-average matrix $M(B)$ is diagonal up to a common factor, and whose innovation
covariance is diagonal. An unrestricted VARMA relaxes all three: full $P(B)$ and
$M(B)$, and an unrestricted $\Sigma$. The transfer function model is therefore the
special case in which the multivariate dynamics are \emph{recursive} and the
contemporaneous structure is \emph{declared} rather than estimated. This is what
makes exact estimation with a diagonal-innovation engine possible, and what the
program trades away relative to a full VARMA: it cannot represent feedback, and it
assumes an innovation covariance that is diagonal unless a covariance is deliberately
freed.

\subsection{What the model generalizes, and what it extends}

In the other direction, the model \emph{generalizes} the univariate ARIMA model with
intervention: a system of one series, with no inputs, is exactly a univariate model,
and the program reproduces the univariate estimates to machine precision
(\S\ref{sec:separability}). It generalizes single-equation dynamic regression by
allowing the regressors to be modelled series with their own dynamics, so that their
pre-sample and their forecasts are handled within the system rather than conditioned
upon.

Relative to the classical transfer function apparatus, the program extends it in two
respects. First, from a single output to a \emph{network}: the single-output theory
\cite{bjr1994,liu1982} is generalized to a directed acyclic graph of transfers
(\S\ref{sec:network}), a recursive dynamic system that the diagonal VARMA
accommodates without modification. Second, from an approximate to an \emph{exact}
likelihood: because each input is a series of the system, its pre-sample is
integrated out by the exact VARMA likelihood rather than backforecast or truncated
(\S\ref{sec:truncation}), so the reported log-likelihood is that of the observed
data and the usual likelihood-based inference is valid.

\subsection{Limitations}

The scope above also delimits what the program does not do. It cannot estimate
feedback systems; it assumes exogenous inputs, an assumption that is testable but not
relaxable within a link; the innovation covariance is diagonal by default, and a
contemporaneous transfer and a freed covariance cannot be identified simultaneously
(\S\ref{sec:identification}); genuinely nonlinear parameter restrictions cannot be
imposed, only tested (\S\ref{sec:gain}); and near a unit-root transfer denominator the
exact cast becomes numerically delicate (\S\ref{sec:mcstudy}). Within these bounds,
the estimation is exact and the classical identification, characterization and
diagnostic apparatus applies unchanged.

\section{Design principles and open limitations}
\label{sec:todo}

\subsection{Principles carried over from the classical methodology}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The order of reformulation.} A misspecified \emph{relation} can make
  the residual ACF and PACF resemble a misspecified \emph{noise}, but a misspecified
  noise cannot make the cross-correlation function resemble a misspecified relation:
  the contamination runs one way, because what is left over from the input ends up
  inside the noise~\cite[chs.~11--12]{bjr1994}. The transfer $\nu(B)$ is therefore
  reformulated to adequacy \emph{before} the noise $\theta(B)$. The program combines
  the two diagnostics accordingly and warns, when both look inadequate, that the
  noise may merely be displaying the relation's residue and should not yet be
  altered.

\item \textbf{The long-run gain and the mean lag} (\S\ref{sec:gain}). Without them
  the relation is estimated but not described.

\item \textbf{Aggregates.} An accounting identity (\textsc{employed} $=$ sum of
  sectors; \textsc{unemployed} $=$ \textsc{active} $-$ \textsc{employed}) does
  \emph{not} belong in the model: it is computed after forecasting, as the legacy
  programs did. What is not trivial is its \emph{band}. The forecast errors of the
  series are correlated---through the network they share innovations---so the
  variance of an aggregate is \textbf{not} the sum of the variances; it is
  $c'Vc$, with $V$ the full forecast error covariance, propagated to levels by the
  delta method.

  Computing it required correcting a defect: the forecast variance assumed $\Sigma$
  diagonal, which ceases to hold once covariances are freed (\S\ref{sec:qq}). On the
  chain $X\rightarrow M \rightarrow Y$ with $b=2$ on the link $M\rightarrow Y$, the
  aggregate's band coincides with the na\"ive independent sum at horizons 1 and
  2---$M$'s innovation has not yet reached $Y$---and separates from horizon 3
  onwards, with
  $\mathrm{sd}(Y-M) < \sqrt{\mathrm{var}(Y)+\mathrm{var}(M)} < \mathrm{sd}(Y+M)$, as
  positive correlation requires. The pure delay appears as the horizon at which the
  correlation is born.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Open limitations}
\label{sec:open}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Nonlinear restrictions cannot be imposed.} The slot table expresses
  free, fixed, shared, product and linear-combination relations
  (\S\ref{sec:restrictions})---linear and multiplicative constraints among
  parameters. A unit long-run gain, $g=\nu(1)=1$, is a genuinely nonlinear
  constraint on the $\omega$'s and $\delta$'s and cannot be imposed by the slot
  table; it can, however, be tested directly (\S\ref{sec:gain}). Imposing it
  requires transforming the data, as in the classical treatment.


\item \textbf{Monitoring without re-estimation.} The recursive machinery of
  \S\ref{sec:recursive} performs the evaluation half of a monitoring workflow
  (\texttt{-R}); the operational half---appending new data to a fitted model and
  testing whether it still holds---is not yet implemented.

\item \textbf{Convergence at near-unit memory.} As shown in \S\ref{sec:mcstudy},
  the embedded cast fails to converge in a minority of replications when a transfer
  denominator approaches a unit root ($\delta\approx0.95$), because the common factor
  of Remark~\ref{rem:common} is then a near-exact cancellation.
\end{enumerate}
