\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\textbf{Nothing in this note is new.} The models are Box and Jenkins's, the
identification procedure is theirs, the exact multivariate likelihood is
Mauricio's, and the methodology is Treadway's. What the note reports is what
happens when that classical apparatus is \emph{re-assembled} on top of an exact
vector ARMA likelihood, in one program, with everything estimated at once:
a handful of engineering decisions turn out to depart from the original
solutions in ways that are measurable, and one of them exposes an
identification hazard that is easy to walk into and easy to miss. We show that
the contemporaneous transfer coefficient $\omega_0$ and the innovation
covariance $\sigma_{12}$ are \emph{exactly} observationally equivalent when the
noise and the input share the same autoregressive operator, and are separated
otherwise only by a signal proportional to the difference between those
operators. In a real case (Spanish CPI and the price of crude oil) that
separating signal is $10\%$ of the main one, and the likelihood develops a flat
ridge along which the fit does not improve while the estimates run to a corner.
Every number below is reproducible from the program's test battery.
\end{abstract}

\section{What is not new}
\label{sec:notnew}

It is worth being blunt about this first, because the rest of the note is only
interesting once it is settled.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The models} are the transfer function models of
  Box \& Jenkins (1970), chapters 10--11 of the current
  edition~\cite{bjr1994}.

\item \textbf{The cast} used here---write $N_t = Y_t - \sum_j \nu_j(B) X_{j,t}$,
  stack $N$ and the inputs into a vector, and note that the resulting system is
  a VARMA with \emph{diagonal} $\Phi$, $\Theta$ and $Q$---is the standard
  multivariate representation of a transfer function model. It is in Tiao and
  Box~\cite{tiaobox1981}, in Hillmer and Tiao, and in Reinsel. It is also
  written out by hand, for a three-variable system, in
  Muñoz Polo (2001)~\cite{munoz2001}, \S2.4.

\item \textbf{The network} of \S\ref{sec:network}---several outputs, a series
  that is at once the output of one relation and the input of another---is a
  \emph{recursive} dynamic simultaneous system: a Wold causal chain. It is what
  Mauricio's \texttt{m6} labour-market models already were in 1996; they were
  simply hand-coded, one \texttt{shootx} at a time.

\item \textbf{The scale normalization} $Q_{11}=1$ of \S\ref{sec:qq} is textbook
  identification under a concentrated scale factor. Mauricio himself states that
  the decomposition $\Sigma = \sigma^2 Q$ is not unique~\cite{mauricio1995,
  mauricio2002}.

\item \textbf{The exact Hessian} of \S\ref{sec:hessian} is ordinary numerical
  practice~\cite{dennis1983}. The code for it was already present in the
  ancestor programs---\emph{commented out}.

\item \textbf{The identification hazard} of \S\ref{sec:identification} is, at
  bottom, the Cowles Commission problem: one cannot free a contemporaneous
  structural coefficient and the innovation covariance at the same time. That is
  eighty years old and is the identification problem of any structural VAR. What
  \S\ref{sec:identification} adds is only a quantitative refinement in the
  transfer-function case, and a diagnostic that follows from it.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
What \emph{is} contributed here is engineering, not science: an automatic
reduction from a univariate model specification to a VARMA structure; a
declarative way of stating parameter restrictions that were previously written
into code by hand; three concrete software-correctness findings about a family
of programs that has been in use for thirty years; and a test battery that pins
the result to the univariate program's output to six decimal places. This note
is therefore a \emph{software and reproducibility} note. It is offered in that
spirit.

\section{The lineage, and the one structural idea}

\subsection{Lineage}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Box \& Jenkins (1970) & the models \\
\quad Jenkins's program & \\
\quad\quad \textsc{taste} (Treadway) & identification and estimation; backforecasting \\
\quad\quad\quad \texttt{fue} & univariate (ARIMA/GSM with intervention) \\
Mauricio (1995, 1997) & \emph{exact} likelihood of a VARMA --- \texttt{elf} \\
\quad \texttt{drvarma} & estimates arbitrary VARMA models \\
\quad\quad \texttt{drtran} & the bridge: \texttt{fue} $\rightarrow$ VARMA $\rightarrow$ \texttt{elf} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Muñoz Polo's dissertation~\cite{munoz2001}, supervised by Treadway, is the
document that fixes the methodology of the school, and it already estimates with
Mauricio's engine (``the software implementing the exact ML algorithm programmed
and designed by Mauricio (1995, 1996, 1997)'', ch.~3). The two halves of the
lineage were therefore already joined in 2001---\emph{by hand}. What
\texttt{drtran} does is automate the joining.

\subsection{The cast}

A transfer function model
\begin{equation}
Y_t \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{\omega_j(B)}{\delta_j(B)}\,B^{b_j}\,X_{j,t}
        \;+\; N_t
\label{eq:tf}
\end{equation}
is recast as a VARMA of $m = 1+k$ series in which
\begin{align}
w_{t,1} &= w^{Y}_t - \textstyle\sum_j \nu_j(B)\, w^{X_j}_t \qquad (\text{the noise } N),\\
w_{t,j+1} &= w^{X_j}_t,
\end{align}
with $\Phi$ and $\Theta$ \emph{diagonal} (each series carries its own ARMA) and
$Q$ diagonal. All of the coupling lives in the \emph{subtracted} transfer, so the
VARMA that the likelihood engine sees has no off-diagonal element anywhere.

Two consequences follow, and they are the whole design:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Mauricio's engine can be used \textbf{unmodified}. In this project any
  discrepancy with the univariate program was treated, without exception, as a
  bug in the bridge. It always was one.
\item The joint fit \textbf{reproduces} the univariate program run separately on
  each series, to six decimals, on six real models
  (deterministic seasonality; airline; generalized seasonality with
  fixed-frequency factors; a fixed AR coefficient). This is the entry gate of the
  test battery, and \S\ref{sec:separability} explains why it must hold.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Six engineering departures, measured}

\subsection{The input model: fixed, or estimated jointly}
\label{sec:separability}

The doctrine is explicit~\cite[\S2.6]{munoz2001}: \emph{``the univariate model of
the input remains unaltered from the beginning to the end of the process.''}
\texttt{drtran} does the opposite: it estimates everything---the output's ARMA,
the input's ARMA, the transfer, the deterministic terms and the means---in a
single maximization.

\textbf{Does it matter? No---and that is the result.} On Spanish CPI
$\leftarrow$ WTI crude:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & input ARMA \emph{fixed} & input ARMA \emph{free} \\
\midrule
$\log L$   & $-718.183933$ & $-718.183933$ \\
$\omega_0$ & $0.016402$    & $0.016402$ \\
$\omega_1$ & $0.010792$    & $0.010792$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Identical. This is not a coincidence. With $Q$ \emph{diagonal} and no feedback,
the joint likelihood \emph{factorizes} as $L(N)\cdot L(X)$, and $L(X)$ does not
involve the transfer. Box and Jenkins's decision to leave the input model alone
is therefore \textbf{not an approximation: it is exactly optimal} in that case.
It is also, precisely, why the joint fit homologates with the univariate program.

The factorization \textbf{breaks the moment $\sigma_{12}$ is freed}---and that is
exactly the case the dissertation~\cite[\S2.4]{munoz2001} points to as the
starting point for specifying a bivariate relation. There, joint estimation stops
being a luxury.

\subsection{$\sigma^2 Q$: the normalization that was missing}
\label{sec:qq}

The engine assumes $a_t \sim N(0,\sigma^2 Q)$ and concentrates $\sigma^2$ out.
Mauricio states in both references that this decomposition is \emph{not unique},
and that this \emph{``raises no problem in the estimation of $E[a_t a_t']$, since
interest lies in the product $\sigma^2 Q$, but not in $\sigma^2$ nor in $Q$ by
itself''}~\cite[p.~474]{mauricio2002}.

\textbf{He is right, and we verified it.} The concentrated criterion
is~\cite[eq.~12]{mauricio2002}
\begin{equation}
F(\beta\mid w) \;=\; \bigl(\tilde{w}' R_W^{-1}\tilde{w}\bigr)\,
                     |R_W|^{1/(mn)},
\label{eq:crit}
\end{equation}
and under $Q\to cQ$ the matrix $R_W$ scales by $c$: the first factor goes as
$1/c$, the second as $c$. \emph{$F$ is exactly invariant.} Since $\sigma^2$ is
concentrated, $\hat\sigma^2(cQ)\cdot cQ = \hat\sigma^2(Q)\cdot Q$: the product
does not move. Estimating the same model with the scale of $Q$ free (as the
ancestor programs do) and with it normalized:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & scale free & $Q_{11}=1$ \\
\midrule
$\log L$                         & $-767.424341$ & $-767.424341$ \\
$\hat\Sigma$                     & $0.062666 \,/\, 68.838114$ & $0.062666 \,/\, 68.838114$ \\
$\mathrm{SE}(\phi_1)$            & $0.062204$ & $0.062204$ \\
$\mathrm{SE}(\mu)$               & $0.028502$ & $0.028502$ \\
$\mathrm{SE}(\log \mathrm{var}_2/\mathrm{var}_1)$ & $\mathbf{0.193083}$ & $\mathbf{0.136399}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The flat direction is \emph{exact}, and it is harmless for $\log L$, for
$\hat\Sigma$, and for the standard errors of $\phi,\theta,\mu,\omega,\delta$. The
damage is confined to \textbf{inference on $Q$ itself}: the scale parameter,
which is \emph{literally} unidentified, receives a respectable-looking
$t=-0.19$ with $p=0.85$; and the variance ratio, which \emph{is} identified,
comes out with a standard error inflated by $42\%$.

\texttt{drtran} normalizes $Q_{11}=1$ for one concrete reason: \textbf{deciding
whether a covariance is zero is a test}, and a test has to be a real one. Nothing
else needed it.

\subsection{Standard errors: exact Hessian, not the accumulated one}
\label{sec:hessian}

The ancestor programs compute standard errors from the \emph{BFGS-accumulated}
Hessian. In all of them the exact alternative (a finite-difference Hessian plus a
Cholesky factorization) is written and \emph{commented out}. It was a 1996 cost
decision that nobody revisited.

A BFGS Hessian is built from the steps the optimizer \emph{takes}; it therefore
never sees the directions in which the optimizer does not move, and produces
finite numbers where there should be none. Two measured consequences: the
univariate program's standard errors are \emph{unstable}---two runs of the same
model, with identical point estimates, report $\mathrm{SE}(\mu)=0.073304$ and
$0.028316$; and the standard errors reported for the nine elements of $Q$ in
\texttt{m6-1} lie along the flat direction of \S\ref{sec:qq}.

Using the exact Hessian, $\mathrm{SE}(\mu)=0.028502$, which is the exact GLS
value.

\subsection{``A single output'' versus the network}
\label{sec:network}

The title of \S2.5 of the dissertation is, literally, \emph{``Representation of
the Single-Output Transfer Model''}. That is the boundary of the classical
apparatus: one output, $k$ exogenous inputs.

The school's real systems are not that. In \texttt{m6} (the Spanish labour
market), \texttt{EU} is an \emph{output} of \texttt{EC} \emph{and} an \emph{input}
to \texttt{EI}:
\begin{center}
\texttt{EC} $\xrightarrow{\;b=2\;}$ \texttt{EU} $\xrightarrow{\;b=1\;}$
\texttt{EI} $\xrightarrow{\;b=1\;}$ \texttt{EP}
\end{center}
\texttt{drtran} generalizes the cast to a \emph{directed acyclic graph} of
transfers, declared in a file: each link subtracts its transfer from \emph{its}
output, so a series may be both a target and a source; the VARMA stays diagonal;
and forecasting walks the graph in topological order, propagating the system's
$\psi$ weights,
\begin{equation}
\Psi_{ij}(B) \;=\; \delta_{ij}\,\psi_i(B)
   \;+\; \sum_{k:\,\mathrm{out}(k)=i} \nu_k(B)\,\Psi_{\mathrm{in}(k),\,j}(B),
\end{equation}
so that the forecast error of a series inherits the innovations of everything
upstream of it, each filtered by the $\nu(B)$ it crosses. A \emph{cycle} is
rejected: the system would be simultaneous and cannot be triangularized by
subtraction.

Measured on a synthetic chain $X \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y$: the network
recovers both transfers and, \textbf{with the same seven free parameters}, beats
the star by $213$ points of log-likelihood ($-1038.6$ vs $-1251.8$). In the star,
the direct link $X\rightarrow Y$ that it is forced to postulate is insignificant
($t=1.45$)---because the effect of $X$ on $Y$ is \emph{indirect}, and only the
network can say so.

\subsection{Restrictions: transform the data, or declare them}

The dissertation imposes long-run neutrality ($g = \nu(1) = 1$, unit long-run
gain) by \emph{transforming the data}, rewriting the model as
\[
Y_t - \ln M1_{t-b} \;=\; \frac{\omega^{*}(B)}{\delta(B)}\,B^{b}\,
\nabla \ln M1_t \;+\; N_t,
\qquad \omega^{*}(B)=\omega(B)-\delta(B),
\]
an elegant solution of its time: the restriction is nonlinear in the parameters,
so it is taken \emph{out} of the optimizer by hand. It has the further virtue of
giving the resulting output an economic reading (real balances).

\texttt{drtran} instead declares restrictions in a \emph{slot table}: every
structural parameter is FREE, FIXED or ALIAS (shared), stated in a file using the
names the program prints. This covers the \emph{linear} restrictions---sharing
and fixing, which are what the dissertation uses for identity-linked
triads---but it does \textbf{not} cover the gain, which is nonlinear. See
\S\ref{sec:todo}.

\subsection{The start of the sample: neither truncation nor backforecasting}
\label{sec:truncation}

The transfer $\sum_k \nu_k X_{t-k}$ needs the input's past, and at $t=1$ there is
none. \textsc{taste} \emph{backforecasts} it, which is Box and Jenkins's solution.
\texttt{drtran} originally \emph{truncated}: it assumed a zero pre-sample input.

We first wrote here that \textsc{taste} did it better. That was wrong, and the
error is instructive. \emph{The exact likelihood makes backforecasting
unnecessary, not merely inferior}---but only if the transfer is inside the model.
Two problems were being conflated:

\begin{description}
\item[The noise's initialisation.] Box and Jenkins backforecast the pre-sample
  \emph{innovations}. The exact likelihood computes this analytically. Obsolete,
  as one would expect after thirty years.
\item[The input's pre-sample values.] A different problem entirely. When the noise
  is built \emph{outside} the engine, as $N_t = w_{Y,t} - \sum_k \nu_k
  w_{X,t-k}$, the engine never sees the inputs: it is handed a noise series that
  is already contaminated at the start of the sample. \textbf{No amount of exactness
  downstream can repair that.}
\end{description}

The fix is not to backforecast. It is to stop subtracting. Writing
\[
\delta(B)\,w_{Y,t}=\omega(B)B^{b} w_{X,t}+\delta(B)N_t,
\qquad \phi_N(B)N_t=\theta_N(B)a_{N,t},
\]
and multiplying through by $\phi_N(B)$ gives a restricted VARMA,
\begin{equation}
\bigl[\phi_N\delta\bigr](B)\,w_{Y,t}
\;-\;\bigl[\phi_N\,\omega\,B^{b}\bigr](B)\,w_{X,t}
\;=\;\bigl[\delta\,\theta_N\bigr](B)\,a_{N,t},
\label{eq:embed}
\end{equation}
whose series are handed to the engine \emph{as they are}. The exact likelihood then
performs the pre-sample initialisation itself. The truncation is not repaired; it
\textbf{disappears}. With several links into one series, multiply by all of their
denominators, $D_i=\prod_k\delta_k$; each row is built independently, so the network
of \S\ref{sec:network} needs nothing new.

\textbf{This is what Mauricio's own \texttt{m6} models do.} Their casting routine
writes \texttt{theta1[1][3][4] = -x5}, \texttt{theta1[2][3][4] = x5*x6}---off the
diagonal, with \emph{products} of structural parameters, which is exactly what
multiplying the polynomials of \eqref{eq:embed} produces. They never had a
truncation problem, and they never backforecast. We had reintroduced, by
subtracting, a problem the exact likelihood had already abolished.

\paragraph{And how much did it matter? Less than we claimed.} With the embedded
cast available (\texttt{-V}), the two can be compared. On 200 paired replications
of a 69-observation sample---\texttt{m6}'s length---with a \emph{rational} transfer
(an infinite impulse response, the worst case):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
cast & bias $\omega$ & RMSE $\omega$ & bias $\delta$ & RMSE $\delta$\\
\midrule
subtract & $+0.0017$ & $0.0491$ & $-0.0066$ & $0.0515$\\
embedded & $\mathbf{-0.0002}$ & $0.0490$ & $-0.0057$ & $0.0513$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The bias of $\omega$ essentially vanishes---it falls by a factor of eight---but the
RMSE improves by less than half a percent. Truncation mattered far less than we
feared. What the embedded cast buys is not accuracy but \emph{correctness}: the
number reported as the log-likelihood is now the exact likelihood \emph{of the
data}, so likelihood-ratio tests and information criteria mean what they say.
Under the subtracting cast, the engine computed an exact likelihood---of the wrong
series.

It costs nothing measurable, it reproduces the univariate program to six decimals
when there is no transfer, and it survives every hard case in the battery (a
generalized-seasonality model, an airline, a seasonal AR, a network). It is not yet
the default only because the default has 200 tests behind it.

\section{An identification hazard}
\label{sec:identification}

This is the one result in the note, and it came out of an experiment that was
about something else.

Estimating Spanish CPI $\leftarrow$ WTI with a \emph{contemporaneous} transfer
($b=0$) \emph{and} freeing the innovation covariance at the same time sends the
fit to an absurd corner:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & $b=0$, $\Sigma$ diagonal & $b=0$, $\sigma_{12}$ free \\
\midrule
$\log L$                  & $-718.184$ & $-718.170$ \\
$\omega_0$                & $0.0164$   & $\mathbf{0.1520}$ \\
$\mathrm{corr}(a_N,a_X)$  & $0$        & $\mathbf{-0.985}$ \\
$t$ of $\sigma_{12}$      & ---        & $\mathbf{-2424}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The likelihood \emph{does not improve} ($\mathrm{LR}=0.029$, against
$\chi^2_1 = 3.84$), yet the estimates fly. This is not an optimizer failure. It is
a nearly flat ridge, and it has an exact explanation.

\paragraph{The prewhitened cross-correlation.} Filter both series by the input's
model: $\alpha_t = \phi_X(B) X_t = a_{X,t}$ and $\beta_t = \phi_X(B) Y_t$.

\begin{description}
\item[(A) Contemporaneous transfer] $Y_t = \omega_0 X_t + N_t$, with $N \perp X$:
\[
\beta_t = \omega_0 a_{X,t} + \phi_X(B) N_t,
\qquad
\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_t,\alpha_{t-k}) =
\begin{cases}
\omega_0\,\sigma_X^2, & k=0,\\[2pt]
0, & k>0 .
\end{cases}
\]
A clean spike at $k=0$.

\item[(B) Innovation covariance] $Y_t = N_t$, with
$\mathrm{cov}(a_{N,t}, a_{X,t}) = \sigma_{12}$:
\[
\beta_t = \phi_X(B)\,\psi_N(B)\,a_{N,t},
\qquad
\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_t,\alpha_{t-k})
 = \sigma_{12}\,\bigl[\,B^k\ \text{coefficient of}\ \phi_X(B)\psi_N(B)\,\bigr].
\]
With AR(1) noise and input this is $\sigma_{12}$ at $k=0$ and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_t,\alpha_{t-k}) \;=\;
\sigma_{12}\;\phi_N^{\,k-1}\,\bigl(\phi_N - \phi_X\bigr),
\qquad k \ge 1 .\;}
\label{eq:tail}
\end{equation}
The \emph{same} spike at $k=0$, plus a tail proportional to
$(\phi_N - \phi_X)$.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Consequence.} If $\phi_N = \phi_X$ the tail in \eqref{eq:tail}
vanishes and the two models are \textbf{exactly observationally equivalent}. This
is not numerical collinearity: it is non-identification. And the closer the two
autoregressive structures, the weaker the identification. The tail relative to
the spike:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
case & $\phi_N$ & $\phi_X$ & $k=1$ & $k=2$ & $k=3$ \\
\midrule
CPI $\leftarrow$ WTI (real) & $0.403$ & $0.299$ & $\mathbf{+0.104}$ & $+0.042$ & $+0.017$ \\
Synthetic                   & $0.300$ & $0.500$ & $-0.200$ & $-0.060$ & $-0.018$ \\
$\phi_N = \phi_X$           & ---     & ---     & $\mathbf{0.000}$ & $0.000$ & $0.000$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

In the real case, \emph{all} of the information separating the two mechanisms is
a signal worth $10.4\%$ of the main one. With 215 observations it cannot be
resolved. Hence the ridge.

\paragraph{Three practical consequences,} all verified:
\begin{enumerate}
\item With $b \ge 1$ the problem disappears: the transfer contributes nothing at
  $k=0$, so $\sigma_{12}$ is cleanly identified. On synthetic data with $b=2$ and
  a true $\sigma_{12}=0$: $t=1.33$, $p=0.18$, correctly insignificant, with the
  $\omega$'s untouched.
\item \texttt{drtran} now \emph{warns} when a link with $b=0$ has its covariance
  freed.
\item It explains the school's practice. \texttt{m6-1} carries off-diagonal
  covariances and \emph{no} contemporaneous structure whatever; and the
  dissertation~\cite[\S2.4]{munoz2001} says that the specification of a bivariate
  relation \emph{``may begin with the modification of the matrix $\Sigma$''}. One
  uses \emph{one} of the two devices, never both. We rediscovered this by walking
  into it.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Characterizing the transfer function: gain and mean lag}
\label{sec:gain}

An estimated transfer function is not \emph{described} until two things are
said: how much the output responds in total, and how long it takes. The
$\omega$'s and $\delta$'s are the parametrization, not the response.

\begin{align}
\text{gain}\qquad
 g &= \nu(1) \;=\; \frac{\omega(1)}{\delta(1)},
 && \omega(1)=\sum_k \omega_k,\quad \delta(1)=1-\sum_j \delta_j
 \label{eq:gain}\\[4pt]
\text{mean lag}\qquad
 \bar{m} &= \frac{\nu'(1)}{\nu(1)}
   \;=\; b \;+\; \frac{\sum_k k\,\omega_k}{\omega(1)}
             \;+\; \frac{\sum_j j\,\delta_j}{\delta(1)}
 \label{eq:mlag}
\end{align}

The gain is the total, permanent response to a unit, permanent change in the
input: \emph{how much}. The mean lag is the centre of gravity of the response in
time: \emph{how soon}. It is the natural measure of the \emph{speed} of the
relation, and it is what the pure delay $b$ alone cannot tell you---$b$ says when
the response \emph{begins}, not where its mass is. Note in \eqref{eq:mlag} that a
denominator does not change the gain's character but \emph{stretches} the
response: it adds $\sum j\delta_j/\delta(1)$ to the mean lag.

Standard errors follow from the delta method with the covariance matrix of the
free parameters, $\mathrm{var}(f) = \nabla f' \, C \, \nabla f$. The gradient is
assembled in the \emph{free} space, which is where $C$ lives: a fixed slot
contributes nothing, and a shared one contributes to its representative---so the
gain of a transfer whose denominator is shared with the input's own AR gets the
right standard error, and not the one that would follow from treating the two as
independent.

Two checks. First, an exact self-test: with $s=0$ and $r=0$ the gain \emph{is}
$\omega_0$ and the mean lag \emph{is} $b$, so the delta method must reproduce
$\omega_0$'s standard error to the last digit---it does ($0.042634$ in both), with
a mean lag of exactly $1.000000 \pm 0$. Second, against synthetic ground truth:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
 & \multicolumn{2}{c}{gain} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{mean lag} \\
\cmidrule(lr){2-3}\cmidrule(lr){4-5}
 & truth & estimate & truth & estimate \\
\midrule
$b=2$, $\omega=(0.8,\,0.4)$              & $1.200$ & $1.192$ & $2.333$ & $2.341$ \\
$b=1$, $\omega=0.6$, $\delta=0.6$        & $1.500$ & $1.467$ & $2.500$ & $2.429$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{This is also how long-run hypotheses should be tested.} The
dissertation imposes unit long-run gain by transforming the data and then tests
it by estimating a second, unrestricted model and comparing likelihoods. With
\eqref{eq:gain} and its standard error, the hypothesis $g=1$ is tested
\emph{directly}, on the model already fitted, with no data transformation and no
second estimation.

\section{An application: the pass-through of crude oil to Spanish CPI is not rational}

Three fits, same output and same input, the first two with \emph{exactly} the same
number of free parameters:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrl}
\toprule
model & free & $\log L$ & adequacy \\
\midrule
$\omega_0/(1-\delta_1 B)$ \quad (rational)      & 17 & $-721.72$ & \textbf{INADEQUATE} ($p=0.033$) \\
$\omega_0 + \omega_1 B$ \quad (two $\omega$'s)  & 17 & $\mathbf{-718.18}$ & adequate ($p=0.196$) \\
$(\omega_0+\omega_1 B)/(1-\delta_1 B)$ \quad (nests the above) & 18 & $-718.18$ & adequate \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The rational form \emph{loses with the same number of parameters}. The third fit,
which nests the second, settles it: $\delta_1 = -0.010$ with $t=-0.055$;
$\mathrm{LR}=0.003$ against $\chi^2_1=3.84$. There is no denominator.

The reason is visible in the impulse weights:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
 & $\nu_0$ & $\nu_1$ & $\nu_2$ & $\nu_3$ & $\nu_4$\\
\midrule
rational        & $0.01680$ & $0.00769$ & $0.00352$ & $0.00161$ & $0.00074$\\
two $\omega$'s  & $0.01640$ & $0.01079$ & $0$ & $0$ & $0$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The data want $\nu_1/\nu_0 \approx 0.66$ \emph{and} $\nu_2 = 0$. A geometric decay
cannot do both. The adequacy test catches it exactly there: the noise still
carries a trace of the input at $k=2$, with $r=-0.14$---negative, because the
rational form puts \emph{too much} weight at that lag.

\paragraph{A methodological warning.} In the \emph{forced} rational fit,
$\delta_1 = 0.458$ with $t=6.40$: apparently overwhelming. It is a mirage. With
the denominator imposed, $\delta$ is the \emph{only} route to any weight at lag
one; its significance says ``there is a response at lag one'', not ``the response
is geometric''. The honest test is the one in the nesting model, and there it
dies.

Characterized by \eqref{eq:gain}--\eqref{eq:mlag}, the relation is
\[
g = 0.027195 \;(0.002453),\qquad
\bar{m} = 0.397 \;(0.044)\ \text{months},\qquad b=0 .
\]
The pass-through of crude to the Spanish CPI is \emph{immediate}---its centre of
gravity is less than half a month---and exhausted within two; it is not a
distributed lag that propagates. The estimate $\omega = (0.0164,\,0.0108)$
reproduces the previously documented values $(0.0154,\,0.0104)$.


\section{The pass-through exercise: does the multivariate model forecast better?}
\label{sec:passthrough}

This is the question the earlier work posed---\emph{does adding the oil price
improve the inflation forecast?}---and answered with a bivariate VAR for three
countries (Spain, France, Germany). The answer there was \emph{no}. But the VAR
was \emph{inadequate}, and for a reason that is worth naming, because it is
structural and not a matter of effort.

\paragraph{Why the VAR could not do it.} A VAR imposes the \emph{same} lag
structure on every equation, with full coefficient matrices. The CPI series do not
want that. Their univariate models---arrived at independently---are:
Spain AR(1) plus harmonics; France a seasonal AR$(1)_{12}$; Germany AR(3) plus a
seasonal AR$(1)_{12}$ plus harmonics. A regular VAR$(p)$ with harmonic
deseasonalization cannot carry those, and it showed: the residual portmanteau
\emph{failed} for France ($p=0.006$) and Germany ($p=0.018$). Raising $p$ to
absorb the seasonality would have introduced off-diagonal terms at high lags and,
with them, spurious feedback from inflation to the oil price. The VAR was caught
between an inadequate noise and a fictitious causality.

The cast of \S2.2 does not have this problem. \textbf{Each series keeps its own
univariate model}---seasonal factors, generalized seasonality, fixed-frequency
factors and all---because the VARMA it produces is diagonal, and the transfer is
unidirectional \emph{by construction}, so there is no order to raise and no
feedback to invent.

\paragraph{The structure is reproduced.} Estimating each CPI against WTI with
$b=0$, $r=0$, $s=1$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrl}
\toprule
 & gain $g=\nu(1)$ & (s.e.) & mean lag & noise diagnostic \\
\midrule
Spain   & $0.0272$ & $(0.0025)$ & $0.40$ & passes ($p=0.46$) \\
France  & $0.0155$ & $(0.0015)$ & $0.43$ & \textbf{passes} ($p=0.20$); the VAR failed ($0.006$) \\
Germany & $0.0127$ & $(0.0021)$ & $0.14$ & marginal ($p=0.03$); the VAR failed ($0.018$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The long-run elasticities reproduce the published ordering and magnitudes (Spain
$2.7\%$, France $1.35\%$, Germany $1.1\%$), and in Germany the \emph{lagged}
coefficient is insignificant ($t=0.97$), also as reported---the German channel is
essentially contemporaneous. \textbf{France's noise now passes.} Germany's
improves but remains marginal; its univariate model is the most demanding of the
three and the relation is the weakest.

\paragraph{The forecast comparison, and why the answer was ``no''.} With the exact
variance decomposition of \S\ref{sec:network} we do not need a rolling experiment
to see the mechanism. Forecast error standard deviation of the stationary
inflation series, univariate versus transfer:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{crrrrrr}
\toprule
 & \multicolumn{2}{c}{Spain} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{France} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{Germany}\\
\cmidrule(lr){2-3}\cmidrule(lr){4-5}\cmidrule(lr){6-7}
$h$ & univariate & transfer & univariate & transfer & univariate & transfer\\
\midrule
 1 & $0.2503$ & $\mathbf{0.2412}$ & $0.1880$ & $\mathbf{0.1701}$ & $0.2295$ & $\mathbf{0.2270}$\\
 2 & $0.2699$ & $0.2804$ & $0.1880$ & $0.1868$ & $0.2297$ & $0.2309$\\
12 & $0.2735$ & $0.2839$ & $0.1880$ & $0.1884$ & $0.2329$ & $0.2330$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At $h=1$ the transfer \emph{helps} in all three: at the forecast origin the oil
price of the previous month \emph{has already been observed}, so $\omega_1 X_{t-1}$
is free information. Beyond $h=1$ it \emph{hurts}: to forecast inflation from oil
one must forecast \emph{oil}, whose innovation variance is some three orders of
magnitude larger than inflation's. That injected uncertainty, propagated through
$\nu(B)$, swamps the reduction in the noise. This is exactly the published verdict,
now with its mechanism made explicit rather than inferred from a rolling MAPE.

\paragraph{And a consequence that follows from it.} If the contemporaneous term is
what forces us to forecast oil, drop it. A model with $b=1$---using only the
\emph{already observed} lag---beats the univariate at \emph{every} horizon for
Spain:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{crrr}
\toprule
$h$ & univariate & transfer $b{=}0$ & transfer $b{=}1$ \\
\midrule
 1 & $0.2503$ & $0.2412$ & $\mathbf{0.2384}$ \\
 2 & $0.2699$ & $0.2804$ & $\mathbf{0.2617}$ \\
12 & $0.2735$ & $0.2839$ & $\mathbf{0.2637}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two caveats, both of which corrected earlier and more sweeping claims of ours.

First, $b=1$ is \emph{not} ``$b=0$ minus the contemporaneous term''. It is a
different model---dropping $\omega_0$ makes the noise absorb the contemporaneous
covariation, so $\sigma_N$ grows. There is a trade-off, and it only clearly pays in
Spain; in France and Germany the two are within $1\%$ of each other.

Second---and this is the point that matters---it is \textbf{not} true that the
contemporaneous term is ``unusable for forecasting''. Of course one uses the
\emph{forecast} of the oil price, and that is exactly what the program does: the
future of the input enters the transfer like any other value. The real difficulty
is that the oil price is very nearly \emph{unforecastable}. Its stationary model is
an AR(1) with $\phi=0.30$ on monthly log differences whose innovation standard
deviation is $8.3\%$, so the one-step forecast carries a signal-to-noise ratio of
about $0.18$, and two steps out, $0.05$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{crrr}
\toprule
$l$ & forecast (from a $+5\%$ month) & s.e. & signal/noise \\
\midrule
1 & $+1.50\%$ & $8.30\%$ & $0.18$ \\
2 & $+0.45\%$ & $8.66\%$ & $0.05$ \\
3 & $+0.13\%$ & $8.69\%$ & $0.02$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

So $\omega_0$ does not contribute \emph{unusable} information; it contributes
almost \emph{no} information and all of the variance. Its variance cost to the
inflation forecast is $\omega_0^2\sigma_X^2 = (0.0164\times 8.30)^2 = 0.019$,
against a total inflation forecast variance of $0.25^2=0.063$---nearly a third of
it, bought with a signal that is mostly noise. The contemporaneous channel is a
real feature of the data (its $t$ statistic is between $6$ and $10$ in all three
countries); it is simply not worth its variance, \emph{unless} one conditions on a
scenario for the oil price rather than forecasting it, which is a different
exercise.

\paragraph{What this is not.} These are \emph{theoretical} forecast error
variances, computed from the fitted models. They say what the models imply, not
what happens out of sample, where parameter uncertainty and structural change---the
2021--22 inflation surge, in this very sample---have their say. A genuine
out-of-sample comparison needs recursive forecasting with the parameters held
fixed. \textbf{That comparison is in \S\ref{sec:recursive}, and it overturns the
conclusion this paragraph was about to draw.} We leave the mechanism here because
it is right---the oil price's own unpredictability is large, and beyond one step
the transfer does import a great deal of the variance of the oil forecast---but it
does not follow, as we assumed it would, that the transfer forecasts worse.


\subsection{The out-of-sample verdict, and why the theory misled us}
\label{sec:recursive}

Everything above rests on \emph{theoretical} forecast error variances. That is not
good enough, and the reason is worth stating plainly, because we walked into it:

\begin{quote}
A model's forecast variance is computed \emph{under the assumption that the model
is true.} Each model grades itself. Comparing two models' theoretical variances is
therefore \textbf{not} comparing the two models.
\end{quote}

The program now does the honest test (\texttt{-R}): estimate \emph{once} on the
first $E$ observations, hold the parameters \textbf{fixed}, and roll the forecast
origin forward one datum at a time, comparing each forecast with what actually
happened. It is what the earlier VAR study did, and what settles the question.

Spanish CPI, training window $1..168$, 37 origins, $H=12$. Ratio of the transfer
model's out-of-sample RMSE of the \emph{level} to the univariate's, next to what
the two models' own theoretical variances predicted:

\section{Impulse response and variance decomposition: is this better than a VAR?}
\label{sec:irf}

The weights $\nu_k$ \emph{are} the impulse response---that is the convenience of a
transfer function. But on their own they are not comparable with anything. Two
things make them so, and both are now reported.

\paragraph{The quantified impulse response.} Each $\nu_k$ with its standard error,
and the \emph{cumulative} response, which is the reply to a \emph{permanent} unit
change and converges to the gain. Both standard errors come from the delta method,
with the gradients obtained from the same recursion that generates the weights:
\[
\nu_t=\omega_{t-b}+\sum_j \delta_j\nu_{t-j}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\frac{\partial \nu_t}{\partial \omega_i}=\mathbb{1}[t-b=i]+\sum_j\delta_j\frac{\partial \nu_{t-j}}{\partial\omega_i},
\quad
\frac{\partial \nu_t}{\partial \delta_i}=\nu_{t-i}+\sum_j\delta_j\frac{\partial \nu_{t-j}}{\partial\delta_i}.
\]
A self-test: when the transfer is a single weight, $\mathrm{SE}(\nu_b)$ must equal
$\mathrm{SE}(\omega_0)$ to the last digit. It does.

\paragraph{The forecast error variance decomposition.} With $\Sigma$ diagonal,
\[
\text{share}_{ij}(l) \;=\; \frac{\sigma_{jj}\sum_{t<l}\Psi^{\text{level}}_{ij}(t)^2}{V_i(l)},
\]
clean and unique, because the innovations are orthogonal and there is nothing to
order. For Spanish CPI against WTI:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{crr}
\toprule
$l$ & own noise & \textbf{WTI} \\
\midrule
 1 & $68.2\%$ & $\mathbf{31.8\%}$ \\
 3 & $49.9\%$ & $\mathbf{50.1\%}$ \\
12 & $44.7\%$ & $\mathbf{55.3\%}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A third of the one-month error, and \emph{half} by three months, is the oil
innovation. Note that this is the same fact as \S\ref{sec:passthrough} seen from
the other side: the oil price contributes a great deal of \emph{variance}. What
\S\ref{sec:recursive} added is that it nevertheless contributes enough
\emph{signal} to be worth it---in Spain.

\paragraph{Is this better than a VAR? Yes, but not for the obvious reason.}
In a VAR the impulse response is \emph{not identified} without an ordering, because
$\Sigma$ is not diagonal: someone has to be shocked first, and the answer changes
with that decision. Here the identification \emph{is} the model---$\nu(B)$ is
estimated directly and $Q$ is diagonal.

It would be comfortable to conclude that the transfer model escapes the
identification problem. \textbf{It does not.} What it does is put the restrictions
where they can be seen:

\begin{itemize}
\item The restrictions---exogenous input, diagonal $\Sigma$---are \emph{declared},
  not buried in an ordering.
\item They are \emph{tested}: the cross-correlation at negative lags is an
  exogeneity test, and the adequacy test asks whether $\nu(B)$ is wide enough.
\item They can be \emph{relaxed}, one covariance at a time.
\item And when they are relaxed next to a contemporaneous transfer, the program
  lands back in \emph{exactly} the VAR's problem---$\omega_0$ and $\sigma_{12}$
  explain the same thing at lag zero (\S\ref{sec:identification})---and it says so
  instead of producing a number.
\end{itemize}

Consistently with that, the variance decomposition is \textbf{not reported} when
$\Sigma$ is non-diagonal. With correlated innovations the decomposition is not
unique: someone must be given the common part, and that requires a Cholesky
ordering. That is the VAR's problem. It is not solved here. It is \emph{avoided}
while $\Sigma$ stays diagonal, and \emph{declared} when it does not.

So the superiority is real but modest, and it is of a particular kind: not fewer
assumptions, but assumptions that are explicit, testable and refusable. To which
must be added the one advantage that is not methodological at all but decided the
pass-through exercise---each series may keep the univariate model it actually
needs (\S\ref{sec:passthrough}).

\section{What the dissertation taught, and what remains}
\label{sec:todo}

\subsection{Three things that reading it put into the program}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The order of reformulation.} The dissertation~\cite[\S2.6]{munoz2001}
  points out an asymmetry that is not obvious: a misspecified \emph{relation} can
  make the residual ACF/PACF look like a misspecified \emph{noise}, but \emph{``a
  misspecified noise cannot give, in the CCF, the impression of a misspecified
  relation. For these reasons, $\nu(B)$ is reformulated until it appears adequate
  \emph{before} reformulating $\theta(B)$.''} The contamination runs one way: what
  is left over from the input ends up \emph{inside} the noise. The program used to
  print both diagnostics and say nothing about which to act on; it now combines
  them and says it, and warns---when both look bad---that the noise may merely be
  showing the relation's leftovers, so it should not be touched yet.

\item \textbf{The long-run gain and the mean lag} (\S\ref{sec:gain}). Without them
  the relation is estimated but not described. This was the most valuable of the
  three.

\item \textbf{Aggregates.} An accounting identity (\textsc{employed} $=$ sum of
  sectors; \textsc{unemployed} $=$ \textsc{active} $-$ \textsc{employed}) does
  \emph{not} belong in the model: it is computed after forecasting, as the legacy
  programs did. What is not trivial is its \emph{band}. The forecast errors of the
  series are correlated---through the network they share innovations---so the
  variance of an aggregate is \textbf{not} the sum of the variances; it is
  $c'Vc$, with $V$ the full forecast error covariance, propagated to levels by the
  delta method.

  Computing it required fixing a real defect: the forecast variance assumed
  $\Sigma$ diagonal, which stopped being true the moment covariances could be
  freed (\S\ref{sec:qq}). The check is pretty. On the chain $X\rightarrow M
  \rightarrow Y$ with $b=2$ on the link $M\rightarrow Y$, the aggregate's band
  \emph{coincides} with the naive independent sum at horizons 1 and 2---$M$'s
  innovation has not yet reached $Y$---and separates from horizon 3 onwards, with
  $\mathrm{sd}(Y-M) < \sqrt{\mathrm{var}(Y)+\mathrm{var}(M)} < \mathrm{sd}(Y+M)$,
  exactly as positive correlation requires. The pure delay itself appears as the
  horizon at which the correlation is born.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Five things that remain open}
\label{sec:open}

These are stated as limitations, not as plans.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Making the embedded cast the default} (\S\ref{sec:truncation}).
  It is exact, it costs nothing, and it survives every case; the only reason it is
  an option is that the subtracting cast is the one with two hundred tests behind
  it.

\item \textbf{Nonlinear restrictions cannot be \emph{imposed}.} The slot table
  expresses FREE, FIXED and SHARED---that is, linear equality between parameters.
  A unit long-run gain, $g=\nu(1)=1$, is nonlinear in the $\omega$'s and
  $\delta$'s, so the dissertation imposes it by transforming the data. With
  \S\ref{sec:gain} that hypothesis can now be \emph{tested} directly, which is
  what one usually wants; but it still cannot be \emph{imposed}, and there are
  cases (estimating subject to a theoretical restriction one is not willing to
  question) where imposing is the point.

\item \textbf{The estimative half of identity-linked triads.} The dissertation
  (\S2.4) does more than compute the identity after the fact: it \emph{restricts
  the deterministic parameters of the three series to satisfy the identity},
  which \emph{``economizes the number of parameters to be estimated''} and makes
  the estimates use the data more efficiently. The slot table can already express
  this---it is exactly what sharing a parameter across series means---but it has
  not been exercised on a real triad.

\item \textbf{Growth rates with their standard deviations.} What an economist
  reads is not only the level, but how much it rises this quarter and how much
  against a year ago, each with a band. The likelihood engine's forecasting
  routine already returns the covariance matrices of those differences
  ($v_2$, $v_3$); the program computes them and \emph{throws them away}.

\item \textbf{New observations without re-estimating}, as a \emph{monitoring}
  workflow. The recursive machinery of \S\ref{sec:recursive} does the evaluation
  half of this (\texttt{-R}); what is missing is the operational half---append data
  to a fitted model and ask whether it still holds.
\end{enumerate}
